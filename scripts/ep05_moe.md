# Episode 5: M o E and conditional compute

Welcome to episode five. So far in this series we have talked about the attention side of the transformer. How attention replaced recurrence, how position embeddings evolved, how the K V cache and flash attention made attention practical at scale. But a transformer block has two parts. Attention, and a feed-forward network. Attention decides where information flows between positions. The feed-forward network, the M L P, processes information at each position independently. And the M L P is actually where most of the parameters live. In a standard transformer, roughly two thirds of the total parameters are in the feed-forward layers, because the M L P's hidden dimension is typically four times the model dimension.

Today we are going to talk about what happens when you replace that single feed-forward network with a collection of them, and only activate a few per token. This is mixture of experts, or M o E. It is arguably the most important architectural change in the transformer ecosystem since the original paper, and it is what powers many of the leading frontier and open-weight models in twenty twenty four and twenty twenty five.

Let me set up the problem M o E solves by connecting to episode two. Scaling laws tell us that bigger models get lower loss, predictably, as a smooth power law. But bigger models also cost more to run. If you double the parameters in a dense model, you roughly double the compute per token, both during training and inference. For a dense model, model capacity and compute cost are locked together.

This creates a tension at deployment time. You want a big model because it is smarter. But you need it to be fast and cheap because you are serving millions or billions of requests. In the dense paradigm, you choose a model size that balances quality against cost, and you live with the tradeoff. As we discussed in episode two, this is part of why the industry overtrained Llama, using a smaller model trained on more data, to push the tradeoff in the direction of cheaper inference.

M o E breaks this tradeoff more fundamentally. The idea is straightforward. Take the feed-forward layer in each transformer block. Instead of one M L P, have N parallel M L Ps, called experts. For each token, a small router network picks the top k of those N experts, typically k equals two out of eight or sixteen experts. Only the selected experts run. The other experts are skipped entirely for that token.

The result. Total parameters scale with N, the number of experts. But per-token compute scales with k, the number of active experts. A model with eight experts and top two routing has roughly eight times the feed-forward parameters of a dense model of the same hidden dimension, but only two times the per-token compute. You get a much bigger model, in the sense of total knowledge capacity, without a proportional increase in how much work each token requires.

Let me be more concrete about the parameter counts. In a dense transformer, the M L P at each layer has two weight matrices. One that projects from the model dimension d up to the hidden dimension, typically four d, and one that projects back down. Total M L P parameters per layer are roughly eight d squared, accounting for both matrices. With N experts, you have N copies of these matrices, so roughly eight N d squared M L P parameters per layer. But at inference, only k experts run, so the active parameters per token are eight k d squared. The ratio of total to active parameters is N over k. With N equals eight and k equals two, you have four times more total parameters than active parameters.

The idea is not new. Mixture of experts was proposed by Jacobs, Jordan, Nowlan, and Hinton in nineteen ninety one, as a way to divide a learning problem among specialized subnetworks. Shazeer and colleagues applied it to L S T Ms in twenty seventeen, the same year as the original transformer paper. The Switch Transformer at Google in twenty twenty one showed it could work at scale in a transformer, using top one routing to just a single expert per token.

But it took until twenty twenty three and twenty twenty four for M o E to become the dominant architecture for frontier models. The reason is partly that the infrastructure caught up, fast interconnects and efficient all-to-all communication became standard, and partly that the scaling pressure became acute enough to demand the capacity-cost decoupling that M o E provides.

The catalyst was Mixtral. In December twenty twenty three, Mistral A I released Mixtral eight by seven B. Eight experts, each roughly seven billion parameters, with top two routing. Total parameters around forty-seven billion. Active parameters per token around thirteen billion. And Mixtral matched or beat Llama two seventy B on most benchmarks. Llama two seventy B is a dense model with seventy billion active parameters per token. Mixtral got comparable quality at less than one fifth the inference cost, because it only activated thirteen billion parameters per token instead of seventy billion.

DeepSeek pushed this much further. DeepSeek V two, released in mid twenty twenty four, used M o E with one hundred and sixty experts. DeepSeek V three, released in late twenty twenty four, used two hundred and fifty-six fine-grained experts with top eight routing, plus two shared experts that every token passes through. Total parameters six hundred and seventy-one billion. Active parameters per token roughly thirty-seven billion. It achieved performance competitive with G P T four class models at a fraction of the inference cost per token.

So M o E lets you separate capacity from cost. But it introduces a new engineering challenge that is central to making it work. Routing.

The router is a small neural network, almost always a single linear layer with no bias, that takes each token's hidden representation as input and produces a score for each expert. The input is the d-dimensional vector from the previous attention layer. The output is an N-dimensional vector of logits, one per expert. Apply softmax to get a probability distribution over experts. Pick the top k. Route the token to those experts, compute the M L P output for each, and combine the results as a weighted sum using the softmax probabilities as gate weights.

The combining step is worth being precise about. If expert three and expert seven are selected for a token, with gate weights zero point six and zero point four, the output is zero point six times the output of expert three plus zero point four times the output of expert seven. The gate weights are the softmax probabilities from the router, restricted to the selected experts and renormalized to sum to one. This means the router not only decides which experts to use but also how much to weight each one.

Now, the central problem. Load balance. Left to itself, the router will collapse. Here is the dynamic. Early in training, by random chance, one expert receives slightly more tokens than the others. More tokens means more gradient updates. More gradient updates means that expert improves faster. An improved expert scores higher in the router's evaluation, so the router sends even more tokens to it. This is a classic positive feedback loop. Within a few thousand training steps, one or two experts handle almost all tokens and the rest are dead weight, never activated, never learning. This is called expert collapse, and it is the central failure mode of M o E training.

The standard fix is a load-balancing auxiliary loss. The construction is specific and worth understanding. For each expert i, compute two quantities. First, f sub i, the fraction of tokens in the batch that were actually routed to expert i. This is a hard count, based on the top-k selection. Second, P sub i, the mean of the router's softmax probability for expert i, averaged across all tokens in the batch. This is a soft quantity, based on the router's full probability distribution, not just the selected experts.

The auxiliary loss is alpha times N times the sum over all experts of f sub i times P sub i. Alpha is a coefficient, typically around zero point zero one. N is the number of experts.

Why this specific form? At perfect balance, f sub i equals one over N for each expert, meaning each expert gets an equal fraction of tokens. And P sub i equals one over N, meaning the router assigns equal probability to each expert on average. The product f sub i times P sub i would then be one over N squared for each expert, and the sum would be one over N. Any deviation from balance increases this sum. The factor of N normalizes the loss so it is independent of the number of experts.

The reason for using the product of f and P rather than, say, just penalizing the variance of f, is that the product is differentiable with respect to the router's parameters through P sub i, even though f sub i, which depends on the hard top-k selection, is not differentiable. The gradient flows through the soft probability P, which nudges the router toward balance.

The coefficient alpha is a knob with a real tradeoff. Too small and experts still collapse. Too large and you are distorting the main training objective. The model spends gradient capacity on balancing experts rather than on learning language. The standard values of zero point zero one to zero point one work for most configurations, but tuning alpha is one of the practical challenges of M o E training.

DeepSeek V three introduced a cleaner alternative. Auxiliary-loss-free balancing. The idea is to maintain a per-expert bias term that influences routing decisions without adding any term to the training loss. For each expert, you track a running count of how many tokens it has received. If an expert is underutilized relative to the expected load, its bias goes up, making the router more likely to send tokens to it. If overutilized, its bias goes down. The bias is added to the router logits before the top-k selection, so it affects which experts are chosen. But critically, the bias is not included in the gate weight computation. The gate weights that determine how much each expert contributes to the output are computed from the original, unbiased softmax. This means the bias affects routing for balance but does not distort the actual model output.

This is elegant because it separates two concerns. Balance is enforced through the routing decision. The model's representational capacity is determined by the unbiased gate weights. The main training loss remains clean.

Let me talk about the infrastructure side, because this is where M o E gets genuinely hard at scale. In a dense model, every G P U computes the same thing for every token. You can shard the model across G P Us with tensor parallelism, splitting each layer's matrices across devices, or pipeline parallelism, assigning different layers to different devices, or data parallelism, replicating the model and splitting the batch. The communication patterns are regular and predictable.

M o E introduces irregular, data-dependent communication. Different tokens go to different experts, and in a distributed training setup, different experts live on different G P Us. Token A needs expert three, which is on G P U two. Token B needs expert seven, which is on G P U five. You need an all-to-all communication step. Gather the tokens from wherever they are in the data-parallel group, route them to the G P U that owns the right expert, compute, and route the results back. This all-to-all is the main systems challenge of M o E at scale. It requires fast interconnects, NVLink within a node and InfiniBand or similar between nodes, careful placement of experts across devices, and sometimes capacity factors that cap how many tokens each expert can accept per batch to prevent extreme communication imbalances.

The Switch Transformer paper introduced the concept of expert parallelism as a distinct parallelism dimension. Each expert lives on its own device, or a set of devices shares the experts using tensor parallelism within each expert. Tokens are dispatched to the right device, processed, and returned. This is conceptually clean but requires at least as many devices as experts, which limits the minimum deployment footprint.

Modern systems, like the Megablocks library, use block-sparse operations to handle variable numbers of tokens per expert more efficiently, avoiding the padding waste that comes from treating all experts as having the same batch size when in reality some receive more tokens than others.

There is a practical nuance about when M o E helps versus hurts. For very small models, the overhead of the router, the load-balancing loss, the underutilized experts early in training, and the all-to-all communication make M o E worse than dense. The crossover point, where M o E starts delivering better quality per F L O P than dense, is generally somewhere above a few billion total parameters. Below that, just use a dense model. Above that, M o E gives you more capacity per F L O P, and the advantage widens as you scale.

Scaling laws for M o E models follow different curves than dense models. You get more performance per unit of training compute, because you are activating more total knowledge without proportionally increasing the per-token F L O P count. But the gains are not free. You need more total memory to store all the expert weights, even though only a fraction are active per token. You need more communication bandwidth. And you need more engineering complexity. M o E trades compute efficiency for memory, communication, and systems engineering.

Let me mention a few architectural variations that show where M o E is headed.

Fine-grained experts. Instead of a few large experts, each the size of a full M L P, use many small experts. DeepSeek V three uses two hundred and fifty-six experts, each much smaller than the standard M L P from a dense model of comparable hidden dimension. The router selects eight of the two hundred and fifty-six. The advantage is finer-grained routing. The router can compose different combinations of small, specialized experts for different tokens, giving it much more flexibility than choosing two out of eight large experts. The disadvantage is more complex routing and potentially more communication, since tokens are spread across more devices.

Shared experts. Some architectures designate one or two experts as shared, meaning every token passes through them regardless of the routing decision. The shared experts handle the common, general-purpose computation that all tokens need. The routed experts add specialized capacity on top. This ensures a baseline quality floor for every token, while still getting the benefits of specialization from the routed experts. DeepSeek V two and V three both use two shared experts alongside the routed ones.

Mixture of depths. A twenty twenty four paper from Google DeepMind. Instead of routing tokens to different experts within a layer, route tokens to either process through the layer or skip it entirely. A router decides, per token per layer, whether this layer should compute on this token or let it pass through via the residual connection unchanged. This extends the M o E idea from "which expert" to "whether to compute at all." Easy tokens that are well-represented can skip layers. Hard tokens get the full computation. This reduces average compute per token while maintaining quality on the hard cases.

Let me step back and frame why M o E is the architecture of the current era. In the dense paradigm, scaling laws told you that bigger models are better, but cost grows linearly with size. The only escape was overtraining smaller models, as we discussed in episode two with Llama. In the M o E paradigm, you can scale total parameters, and therefore capacity, much faster than per-token cost. DeepSeek V three has six hundred and seventy-one billion parameters but runs at roughly the cost of a forty billion parameter dense model. That is roughly a fifteen-to-one ratio between total capacity and active cost. This ratio is why the frontier moved so fast in twenty twenty four. M o E let labs build models that were effectively much bigger than their inference cost suggested.

There is a philosophical point here too. Dense models assume every parameter should be used for every token. M o E says, most of what a model knows is irrelevant to any given token, so only activate the relevant parts. This is conditional computation. It mirrors how expertise works in other domains. A doctor does not activate their knowledge of constitutional law when diagnosing a patient. A programmer does not activate their knowledge of French cooking when writing a sort algorithm. M o E lets the model do the same, maintaining a broad base of knowledge while only paying the compute cost for the slice that is relevant right now.

If I had to summarize in one sentence. M o E decouples model capacity from inference cost by routing each token through a small subset of specialized experts, and this decoupling is what made frontier models economically viable at the scale the field demanded in twenty twenty four and twenty twenty five.
