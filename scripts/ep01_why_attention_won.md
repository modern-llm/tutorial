# Episode 1: Why attention won

Welcome to episode one. This series is for someone who already knows transformers, attention, and the basics of pretraining, and who wants to close the gap on what changed in large language models after twenty twenty three. We are going to start at the beginning, because the choice the field made in twenty seventeen, to throw out recurrence and bet everything on attention, is what made the rest of this story possible.

Today's question. What was wrong with recurrent neural networks that self-attention solved? Or more precisely, what did the field gain by walking away from recurrence, and what did we pay for it?

Picture sequence modeling around twenty fourteen, twenty fifteen. You wanted to translate a sentence, summarize a document, generate text. The default tool was an L S T M, the long short-term memory network. Recurrent networks had been around since the eighties, but L S T Ms had finally cracked the vanishing gradient problem. By twenty fourteen, encoder decoder L S T Ms powered Google Translate. By twenty fifteen, sequence to sequence models were the obvious answer for almost any sequence task. Andrej Karpathy's blog post called The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks was the moment a lot of people realized you could just throw text at one of these and get something coherent out. It felt like a solved direction.

But under the hood, recurrence had a deep, structural problem. To compute the hidden state at position t, you needed the hidden state at position t minus one. Which needed t minus two. Which needed t minus three. Step by step. There is no way around it. A sequence of length one thousand requires one thousand sequential operations. You cannot start computing the second word's representation until you finish the first.

This sounds like a small thing. It is not a small thing. It is the central thing.

Modern accelerators, G P Us in twenty fifteen, T P Us by twenty seventeen, are built around one trick. Do thousands of operations at the same time. Wide matrix multiplies, vector additions, reductions, all happening in parallel across thousands of cores. If you give a G P U a workload that has a sequential dependency, it twiddles its thumbs. Most of the chip sits idle, waiting for the previous step to finish.

So when you trained an R N N on a thousand-token sequence, you were not really using your hardware. You were using a tiny slice of it, one timestep at a time. Researchers worked around this with bigger batch sizes. Process many sequences in parallel, even if each individual sequence is sequential. But you ran out of memory long before you ran out of compute. The hardware was the bottleneck, and recurrence was holding it back.

There was a second problem. Long-range dependency. An L S T M can in principle carry information across long distances, but in practice the gradient signal degrades over many timesteps. If word number five depends on word number five hundred, the signal that connects them has to travel through four hundred and ninety five L S T M cells, and each one mangles it a little. People published results in twenty fifteen showing that L S T Ms effectively lose track around a few hundred tokens.

So you had two coupled problems. Sequential compute starved your hardware. And the architecture itself was bad at long-range dependencies.

The first attempt to fix this came from a different direction. Convolutional sequence models. Papers like ByteNet and WaveNet, and the convolutional sequence-to-sequence work from Facebook A I Research, argued, replace recurrence with stacked convolutions, and now you can compute every position in parallel within a layer. They worked, sort of. They were faster than L S T Ms. But they came with their own problem. A single convolution only sees a few neighbors, so to model long range you need many layers, and information still has to traverse depth. The path length between two distant tokens, measured in layers, was logarithmic at best.

Meanwhile, attention had been quietly creeping in as an add-on. In twenty fourteen, the Bahdanau paper introduced attention as a fix for one specific issue in R N N encoder decoders for translation. The encoder L S T M had to compress the entire source sentence into a single fixed-size vector, its final hidden state, and the decoder had to translate from that. For long sentences, this was a brutal bottleneck. The Bahdanau paper said, instead of forcing one vector to carry everything, let the decoder look back at all the encoder's hidden states, with weights that depend on what the decoder is trying to produce right now. Compute a weighted average over the encoder states, with the weights changing per output position.

This was attention. But it sat on top of the recurrence. The encoder was still an L S T M. The decoder was still an L S T M. Attention was just a smarter pooling operation in between.

Over twenty fifteen and twenty sixteen, attention became standard in machine translation, captioning, every sequence task. People made it more sophisticated. There was multi-headed attention, dot-product attention, content-based attention. The pattern in the field was, keep the L S T M, bolt on better attention.

Then in June of twenty seventeen, the Vaswani paper at Google Brain, called Attention Is All You Need, proposed something more radical. Throw out the recurrence entirely. Just stack attention layers, plus simple feed-forward networks and residual connections, and that is your model. The key insight. Attention by itself is enough to build the kind of representations that L S T Ms were giving you, and crucially, attention is parallel.

Let me explain why parallel. In an attention layer, each position computes a query vector, a key vector, and a value vector. All just linear projections of its input. Then, to figure out what each position should pay attention to, you take the dot product of its query with every position's key. That gives you a score for every pair. Softmax those scores, multiply by the values, sum it up. The output for position five only depends on the queries, keys, and values that already exist. It does not depend on the output for position four. So you can compute every output position simultaneously. The entire layer is one big matrix multiplication. G P Us love it.

Let me slow down on what the query, the key, and the value actually do, because this abstraction is going to keep showing up across the rest of this series. The cleanest framing I have heard for it is a soft database lookup. Every position in the sequence projects its input into three vectors, by multiplying its embedding by three different learned weight matrices. The query is the search signal, what this position is looking for. The key is the index, what this position offers to others trying to match against it. The value is the actual content this position will hand over once it has been matched.

A library analogy makes the value piece concrete. Imagine you are at a library looking for material. Your query is your research question. The key for each book is what is written on its spine and on its catalog card, the title and the subject tags. That is the surface another token uses to decide whether this book is worth opening. The value is what is actually written inside the book once you open it. And the crucial thing is that key and value are two separate learned projections of the same input. The model is allowed to advertise a token one way for the purpose of being found, and contribute something different once it has actually been attended to. That separation is part of why attention is so flexible. A token can flag itself as relevant on one dimension and then deliver content along a totally different dimension.

To update position five's representation, you take the dot product of position five's query with every key in the sequence. Dot product is the similarity measure here. If two vectors point in similar directions, the score is high. Softmax across all those scores turns them into a probability distribution over positions. Then the new representation for position five is a weighted average of every value vector in the sequence, weighted by that distribution. Position five has, in effect, scanned the entire sequence, decided which positions matter most, and pulled in their content. That is the whole operation, in one layer, in one pass.

A natural question is, if every attention head runs this same lookup, why bother with more than one? The original transformer used eight heads in parallel, each with its own query, key, and value projections, each starting with different random weights. The outputs of all eight heads get concatenated and then mixed by one final linear layer. The gradient flowing back into those heads has a strong reason to push them apart. If two heads learn the same thing, the model is wasting capacity, which the loss function does not love. So during training the heads diverge. What people found, when they later visualized trained transformers, is what is called emergent specialization. One head ends up tracking subject verb agreement across long distances. Another head behaves like a local window, only looking at the previous one or two tokens. Another head learns coreference, linking pronouns back to the nouns they refer to. Nobody told the model to organize itself this way. Random initialization plus a loss that punishes redundancy is enough to drive each head into its own subspace of the data. You get a small ensemble of specialized lookups for free, which is part of why attention generalizes as well as it does.

There is one more structural detail about the transformer that is worth covering now, because it becomes important in almost every later episode. A transformer block is not just an attention layer. It is an attention layer followed by a feed-forward network, also called the M L P. The attention layer decides where information flows between positions. The M L P processes the information at each position independently, in parallel. Concretely, the M L P is two linear transformations with a nonlinearity in between. It projects the representation up to a wider dimension, typically four times the model dimension, applies an activation function, and projects back down. Simple. But the M L P is actually where most of the parameters live. In a standard transformer, roughly two thirds of the total parameters are in the feed-forward layers. There is a growing body of evidence, starting with work by Geva and colleagues in twenty twenty one, that the M L P layers act as a kind of key-value memory, storing factual associations learned during pretraining. The attention layers route information. The M L P layers store and retrieve knowledge. Both are critical, but their roles are different.

These two components, attention and M L P, do not operate in isolation. They read from and write to a shared residual stream. The residual stream is the vector at each position that flows through the entire depth of the model, accumulating contributions from each layer. Each attention layer reads from the stream, computes its output, and adds it back to the stream. Each M L P does the same. The residual connection, the "add" in "add and normalize," is what makes this work. It means each layer only needs to contribute a small delta to the representation, rather than reconstructing the entire thing from scratch. This residual stream perspective, formalized by Elhage and colleagues at Anthropic in twenty twenty one, reframes the transformer as a shared highway that layers read from and write to. It is a useful mental model for understanding how information moves through a deep transformer.

One more piece. Between each sublayer, the transformer applies layer normalization. The original paper applied normalization after the attention and after the M L P, a pattern called post-norm. Modern transformers almost universally use pre-norm instead, normalizing the input to each sublayer before the computation. Pre-norm makes training much more stable at large scale, because it controls the magnitude of the inputs to each layer regardless of what earlier layers did. The shift from post-norm to pre-norm was not in the original twenty seventeen paper. It emerged empirically over twenty nineteen and twenty twenty as people tried to train deeper and larger models and found that post-norm training was unstable. It is a small architectural detail with large practical consequences.

One more detail that connects episode one to episode two. During training, the model is predicting the next token at every position simultaneously. But if position five can attend to position six, it can cheat, it can just copy the answer instead of learning to predict it. So there is a causal mask, a triangular matrix that sets attention scores to negative infinity for all positions ahead of the current one. After the softmax, those positions get zero weight. Position five can attend to positions one through five, but not six or beyond. This is what makes the architecture autoregressive. It is also what makes parallel training work. Every position predicts its next token at the same time, and the mask prevents information leakage from the future. At inference time, when you generate one token at a time, the mask is implicit, there are no future tokens to mask. But during training, the mask is what allows you to compute the loss at every position in a single forward pass.

That is pillar one of why attention won. Parallel computation across the sequence.

Pillar two is path length. In an R N N, to mix information from position one into position one thousand, the signal goes through nine hundred and ninety nine recurrent steps. In an attention layer, position one thousand directly takes the dot product with position one's key. The path length is one. Through one attention layer, every position can attend to every other position. Information flows globally, in one shot, in one layer. Long-range dependencies stop being an architectural struggle.

Pillar three is what you might call architectural humility. Recurrent and convolutional models bake in strong structural assumptions about how a sequence should be processed. Left to right, with strong locality. The transformer almost has no inductive bias about sequence structure. Position information has to be added explicitly, with position embeddings, because the attention layer itself treats the input as an unordered set. People worried about this at first. They thought less inductive bias meant the model would need much more data to learn anything. And it did need more data. But it turned out that with enough data, this was not a bug. It was the entire point. Lower inductive bias means the model can learn structure from data, instead of having structure imposed on it. Which means as you scale up the data, the model keeps getting better, instead of saturating against an assumption that no longer fits the data distribution.

None of this was free. Self-attention is quadratic. Every position attends to every other position, so a sequence of length n requires n squared operations and n squared memory. For a sequence of length one thousand, that is one million attention scores per head. For ten thousand, a hundred million. For a hundred thousand, ten billion. This is why long context was a hard problem for years. And why so much of the engineering work in twenty twenty three and twenty twenty four, flash attention, sliding window attention, key-value cache compression, is about working around quadratic attention. We will come back to this in episode four.

Self-attention also gave up the locality bias that convolutions had. The first transformer paper buried that. But over time, models with sliding-window attention, with relative position encodings, with attention sinks, brought back the parts of locality that were actually useful, on top of a foundation of full attention.

The deeper trade was this. Recurrence and convolution gave you efficient local computation, but a hard ceiling on what you could learn. Self-attention gave you uniform expressiveness. Any token can talk to any token, at the cost of brutal scaling. The bet the field made in twenty seventeen was that the second problem was an engineering problem, and the first was a science problem. Engineering problems get solved. Science problems are harder.

If I had to compress all of this to one sentence. Attention won because it converted a sequential algorithm into a parallel algorithm without losing, and in fact gaining, modeling power. Everything that has happened in language models since twenty seventeen depends on that conversion. The reason we can train hundred-billion parameter models in months instead of decades is parallel attention. The reason we can keep scaling is that attention's bottleneck is hardware, and hardware keeps getting faster. The reason your reasoning model can backtrack and self-correct in a long chain of thought is that in twenty seventeen, the architecture stopped imposing where information could flow.

In the next episode, we will look at what happened when people pointed massive amounts of compute at this architecture and just asked it to predict the next token. We will talk about scaling laws, the empirical observation that bigger models trained on more data keep getting better, in a startlingly predictable way. And Chinchilla, the twenty twenty two paper that rewrote what we thought we knew about how to spend training compute. The plot twist there is that for years, we were training models that were too big and too underfed.
