# Episode 7: Lo R A and cheap adaptation

Welcome to episode seven. For the first six episodes we have been building up the modern model stack. Attention, scaling, position encodings, inference efficiency, mixture of experts, and long context. Before we move into post-training, I want to cover the adaptation layer that made large models practical for ordinary teams. How do you actually fine-tune a large model in practice, when full fine-tuning is too expensive?

The naive answer is, the same way you pretrained it. Take all the parameters, compute gradients, update them with the optimizer. This is called full fine-tuning. And for small models, it works fine. But for modern large models, full fine-tuning has a serious practical problem. It is expensive. Not just in compute, but in memory.

Let me make this concrete. When you fine-tune a model with Adam W, you need to store, for every parameter, the parameter itself, the gradient, the first moment estimate, and the second moment estimate. That is four copies of every parameter. For a seven billion parameter model in sixteen-bit precision, the parameters alone take fourteen gigabytes. The optimizer states add another twenty-eight gigabytes. Total memory just for the trainable state is around forty-two gigabytes, before you account for activations, the K V cache, or the batch of data. A seventy billion parameter model needs over four hundred gigabytes of optimizer state. That does not fit on any single G P U. You need multi-G P U setups with sophisticated parallelism just to fine-tune.

And here is the practical issue. Most people who want to fine-tune a model do not have a cluster of eight A one hundred G P Us. They have one or two consumer G P Us, maybe a rented cloud instance. Full fine-tuning is out of reach for them. And even for organizations that can afford the hardware, running a full fine-tuning job for every task, every experiment, every customization is wasteful if a cheaper method works nearly as well.

This is the problem that parameter-efficient fine-tuning solves. Instead of updating all the parameters, update a small number of additional parameters while keeping the base model frozen. The base model provides the foundation. The small set of trainable parameters provides the task-specific adaptation. The total trainable parameter count drops from billions to millions, the memory footprint shrinks proportionally, and the storage cost of each fine-tuned variant is just the small set of new parameters, not a full copy of the entire model.

There were several approaches before Lo R A. Let me mention the important ones briefly because they set the stage.

Adapters, proposed by Houlsby and colleagues in twenty nineteen, insert small trainable bottleneck layers between the existing transformer layers. Each adapter is a down-projection, a nonlinearity, and an up-projection, with a residual connection. The adapter dimensions are small, so the total trainable parameters are a small fraction of the model. This works, but it adds extra layers to the forward pass, which increases inference latency. Every forward pass goes through the adapter, even after training is done.

Prefix tuning, by Li and Liang in twenty twenty one, prepends a set of trainable "virtual tokens" to the input. These virtual tokens have learnable embeddings that are optimized during training while the model is frozen. The model attends to these virtual tokens along with the real input, and the virtual tokens steer the model's behavior. This is clever but limited in expressiveness, and the virtual tokens consume part of the context window.

Prompt tuning is a simplified version of prefix tuning where only the embeddings of the virtual tokens are trained, without any reparameterization. It works surprisingly well for large models but less well for smaller ones.

Then in twenty twenty one, Hu and colleagues at Microsoft published Lo R A, Low-Rank Adaptation. It quickly became the dominant method, and it remains the default approach for fine-tuning large models in twenty twenty five. Let me explain why.

The key insight behind Lo R A is an empirical observation about what happens during fine-tuning. When you full-fine-tune a pretrained model on a downstream task, the weight matrices change. You start with weight matrix W and end with W plus delta W, where delta W is the update. Aghajanyan and colleagues, also at Microsoft, showed in twenty twenty one that these updates occupy a surprisingly low-rank subspace. If you compute the singular value decomposition of delta W, most of the singular values are near zero. The update can be well-approximated by a matrix of rank much smaller than the full dimension.

What does low rank mean concretely? A d-by-d matrix has d squared parameters. A rank r approximation of that matrix can be written as the product of two smaller matrices, one d-by-r and one r-by-d. The total parameters drop from d squared to two times d times r. If d is four thousand and r is eight, that is a reduction from sixteen million parameters to sixty-four thousand. A factor of two hundred and fifty.

Lo R A exploits this directly. For each weight matrix W in the model that you want to adapt, add a parallel low-rank branch. The branch consists of two small matrices, A with shape d-by-r and B with shape r-by-d, initialized so that A is random Gaussian and B is zero. The zero initialization means the branch contributes nothing at the start of training, so the model begins exactly where the pretrained model left off. During fine-tuning, only A and B are trained. W is frozen.

The forward pass computes W times x plus alpha times B times A times x, where x is the input, and alpha is a scaling factor, typically alpha over r, that controls the magnitude of the adaptation relative to the base model. The output is the sum of the frozen base model's output and the low-rank adaptation.

Why does this work so well? There are a few complementary explanations. First, the empirical observation. Fine-tuning updates are genuinely low-rank. The model does not need to change drastically to adapt to a new task. It needs to adjust a few important directions in weight space, and a low-rank update captures those directions. Second, the frozen base model provides a strong regularizer. You cannot overfit as easily when most of the model is frozen. The small number of trainable parameters acts as a natural capacity constraint. Third, the pretrained model already has most of the knowledge needed for the task. Fine-tuning is not building capability from scratch. It is steering existing capability toward a specific application.

The rank r is a hyperparameter that controls the tradeoff between adaptation capacity and efficiency. Common values are four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two, and sixty-four. For many tasks, rank eight is enough. For more complex adaptations, rank thirty-two or sixty-four can help. Going beyond sixty-four rarely improves results, because the update truly is low-rank. You are not leaving capacity on the table by using a small rank.

Which weight matrices should you apply Lo R A to? The original paper experimented with applying it only to the attention projection matrices, W Q and W V. Later work showed that applying Lo R A to all linear layers in the transformer, including the K, O, and M L P projections, generally works better, especially at small ranks. The intuition is that distributing the adaptation across all layers gives the model more flexibility than concentrating it in a few.

Now here is the property that gives Lo R A its killer advantage over adapters and prefix tuning. At inference time, you can merge the Lo R A weights back into the base model. Compute W prime equals W plus alpha times B times A. Replace W with W prime. Delete A and B. The merged model is a standard transformer with no extra layers, no extra computation, no extra latency. The adaptation has zero inference overhead.

This is huge for deployment. You can have one base model and dozens or hundreds of Lo R A adapters for different tasks, different customers, different languages. Each adapter is a small file, typically tens of megabytes instead of tens of gigabytes. You can merge the appropriate adapter on the fly, or serve the base model and swap adapters per request.

This per-request adapter swapping is exactly what S-Lo R A, published in twenty twenty three, implements. The system keeps the base model weights in G P U memory permanently. Lo R A adapter weights are stored in C P U memory and paged into G P U memory as needed, similar to the paged attention technique we discussed in episode four. A request comes in for customer A, the system loads customer A's adapter. The next request is for customer B, swap adapters. The base model computation is shared. Only the small adapter differs. This lets you serve hundreds of personalized models on hardware that can only fit one.

Now let me talk about QLo R A, because it made Lo R A accessible to an even wider audience. QLo R A, published by Dettmers and colleagues in twenty twenty three, combines two ideas. First, quantize the frozen base model to four-bit precision using a technique called Normal Float four, or N F four, which is a data type specifically designed for the distribution of neural network weights. The base model that would normally require fourteen gigabytes at sixteen bits now requires about three and a half gigabytes at four bits. Second, apply Lo R A adapters on top of the quantized base, with the adapters themselves in sixteen-bit precision.

The result is that you can fine-tune a seven billion parameter model on a single consumer G P U with sixteen gigabytes of memory. You can fine-tune a seventy billion parameter model on a single A one hundred with eighty gigabytes. QLo R A made it possible for individual researchers, students, and small companies to fine-tune frontier-scale models on hardware they already had. The quality compared to full fine-tuning is remarkably close. On most benchmarks, QLo R A matches full sixteen-bit fine-tuning within one to two percent, despite the base model being in four-bit precision.

There is a subtlety about how the gradients flow in QLo R A. The forward pass goes through the four-bit weights, but the gradients are computed with respect to the sixteen-bit Lo R A parameters. The four-bit base weights do not receive gradients. They are frozen and quantized. The Lo R A parameters receive full-precision gradients. This means the adaptation is learned in full precision even though the base model is heavily compressed. The base model provides a rough but sufficient foundation. The Lo R A parameters provide the precise task-specific adjustment.

Let me connect Lo R A to two other topics in this series.

First, the connection to episode sixteen, which will cover the Muon optimizer. Lo R A's premise is that fine-tuning updates are low-rank. Muon's premise is that gradient updates should be orthogonalized to respect the matrix structure of weights. Both are making the same fundamental observation. Weight matrices are matrices, not bags of numbers, and the structure of updates to those matrices matters. Lo R A says the updates live in a low-dimensional subspace. Muon says the updates should have equalized singular values. These are complementary views of the same underlying reality, that matrix-valued parameters have geometric structure that element-wise methods ignore.

Second, the connection to the post-training episodes that follow. When we talk about S F T, D P O, and G R P O in the next three episodes, keep in mind that in practice, many teams run these procedures with Lo R A rather than full fine-tuning. You can do S F T with Lo R A, adapting the base model to follow instructions with minimal memory. You can do D P O with Lo R A, though the reference model computation is trickier since you need the frozen reference to not include the adapter. You can even do R L with Lo R A, though the interaction between low-rank updates and policy gradient is less well-studied. The practical impact is that Lo R A democratized the entire post-training pipeline, not just S F T.

One more thing. There are several Lo R A variants worth knowing by name. Lo R A plus adjusts the learning rates of A and B separately, based on the observation that they have different optimal learning rates. Do R A, Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation, decomposes the weight update into a magnitude component and a direction component, applying Lo R A only to the direction. This more closely mimics the behavior of full fine-tuning and often gives slightly better results. AdaLo R A adaptively allocates rank across different weight matrices during training, giving more rank to layers that need more adaptation and less to layers that are already well-suited. These variants improve on the original by small margins, but the original Lo R A remains the most widely used because of its simplicity.

If I had to compress this episode to one paragraph. Fine-tuning a large model by updating all parameters is expensive and often unnecessary. Lo R A freezes the base model and trains a small low-rank decomposition alongside each weight matrix, capturing task-specific adaptations with a fraction of the parameters. QLo R A extends this by quantizing the frozen base to four bits, making it possible to fine-tune a seventy billion parameter model on a single G P U. At inference, Lo R A weights merge into the base model with zero overhead. The technique democratized post-training and made it practical for anyone with a G P U to customize a frontier model.
