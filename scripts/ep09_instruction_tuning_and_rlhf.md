# Episode 9: Instruction tuning and R L H F

Welcome to episode nine. For the first two arcs of this series we have been talking about how to build and adapt the modern model stack. Attention. Scaling laws. Position encoding. Inference efficiency. Mixture of experts. Long context. Lo R A. Multimodal inputs. All of that gives you a model that is very good at one thing. Predicting the next token.

But a raw pretrained model is not a useful product. If you type "write me a Python function to sort a list" into a pretrained model, it might complete the sentence with "that is both efficient and elegant, according to a Stack Overflow post from twenty nineteen." It is continuing the text, not answering you. It has no concept of instructions, no sense that it is in a conversation, no idea that it should help rather than simply predict. It is a next-token machine, and that is all pretraining taught it to be.

Today we are going to talk about the post-training pipeline that turns that raw model into something that follows instructions, has a coherent personality, refuses harmful requests, and generally behaves like what you expect from Chat G P T or Claude. The pipeline has three stages. Supervised fine-tuning, or S F T. Training a reward model. And reinforcement learning, specifically proximal policy optimization, or P P O. Together this is the R L H F pipeline, reinforcement learning from human feedback, and it is the thing that made the chatbot era possible.

Let me start with supervised fine-tuning. The concept is simple. You take your pretrained model and fine-tune it on a curated dataset of instruction-response pairs. "What is the capital of France?" followed by "The capital of France is Paris." "Write a haiku about rain." followed by a haiku. "Explain quantum computing to a five-year-old." followed by a simple explanation. The prompts and responses are formatted as conversations, with explicit role markers for user and assistant.

The training objective is the same as pretraining, next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss. But now you only compute the loss on the assistant's tokens, not the user's tokens. The model learns to generate the response given the prompt. The data is different from pretraining in a crucial way. Instead of raw Internet text with no structure, you are training on examples of the behavior you want. The model learns to complete prompts in an instruction-following style rather than a web-text-continuation style.

This sounds almost too simple to be interesting. And in one sense it is. S F T is just supervised fine-tuning, a technique as old as transfer learning. But two things made it transformative.

First, the quality and format of the data matter enormously, and a surprisingly small amount of good data is enough. The InstructG P T paper from OpenAI in early twenty twenty two used about thirteen thousand human-written demonstrations. Stanford's Alpaca in twenty twenty three used fifty-two thousand examples generated by G P T three point five. The LIMA paper from Meta, also twenty twenty three, showed that as few as one thousand carefully curated examples could produce a highly capable instruction-following model. The title of the LIMA paper was Less Is More for Alignment, and the finding was striking. A model trained on trillions of tokens of raw text, fine-tuned on one thousand good instruction examples, suddenly follows instructions fluently. The base capability was always there. S F T just surfaces it.

Second, S F T defines the model's format and persona. The chat template, the system prompt, the turn-taking structure, the tone and style. When you fine-tune on data formatted as conversations with user turns and assistant turns, the model learns that format. This is why Chat G P T responds in conversation style. It is not because the base model understands conversation. It is because S F T data was formatted that way. If you fine-tuned the same base model on data formatted as academic papers, it would write like an academic paper.

But S F T has a fundamental limitation. It teaches the model what good outputs look like by example, but it does not teach the model to distinguish good outputs from bad ones on a continuous scale. S F T gives the model one correct answer per prompt. What if there are multiple reasonable answers, but some are better than others? What if the model generates something that is fluent and plausible but subtly wrong, or subtly harmful? S F T treats the training data as the gold standard and has no mechanism to penalize outputs that are almost right but actually wrong. It either matches the training distribution or it does not.

This is where the reward model comes in. Stage two of the R L H F pipeline.

A reward model is a separate neural network, usually initialized from the same pretrained base model, that takes a prompt and a response as input and outputs a single scalar score. Higher score means better response. The reward model is not a language model in the generative sense. It does not produce text. It reads text and assigns a number.

To train it, you collect comparison data from human labelers. Given a prompt, you generate two or more candidate responses, typically from the S F T model or from earlier versions of the system. You show these to a human. The human ranks them. "Response A is better than response B." These preference pairs become the training data for the reward model.

The training loss comes from the Bradley-Terry model of pairwise preferences, a framework borrowed from sports ranking that dates to the nineteen fifties. For a pair where response w was preferred over response l, the loss is the negative log of the sigmoid of the score difference, r of w minus r of l. Minimizing this loss pushes the reward model to assign a higher score to the preferred response. The sigmoid means the model only needs to get the ordering right, not the exact magnitude. If response w is much better, the score difference will be large and the loss small. If it is a close call, the score difference will be small and the loss will reflect the uncertainty.

Why not just train on the preference data directly, instead of building a separate reward model? Two reasons. First, human labeling is expensive. Each labeled pair costs real money and time. A reward model generalizes beyond the specific pairs it was trained on. It learns a continuous scoring function that can evaluate any new response, not just the ones humans compared. You extract much more signal per labeled pair. Second, the R L stage needs to evaluate many candidate responses per training step. You need a fast, automated scoring function that can be called millions of times. You cannot have a human in the loop at that frequency.

Architecturally, the reward model is typically the same transformer as the policy, initialized from the same pretrained weights. The main modification is that the language model head, the final layer that produces a probability distribution over tokens, is replaced with a scalar output head, a single linear layer that maps the last hidden state to one number. During training, you feed the prompt plus the response through the model, take the hidden state at the final token position, and project it to a scalar. That scalar is the reward. The model inherits the base model's understanding of language and content, which gives it a strong starting point for evaluating response quality.

A subtlety about what the reward model learns. It is not scoring a single dimension like correctness. It is learning a composite preference function that reflects everything the human labelers cared about, whether they were told to or not. Is the response helpful? Is it accurate? Is it safe? Is it well-formatted? Is it the right length? Is the tone appropriate? The labelers were given guidelines covering these dimensions, but their implicit preferences also leak in. If labelers tend to prefer longer, more detailed responses, the reward model learns to score longer responses higher, regardless of whether the extra length adds value. If labelers prefer responses that sound confident, the reward model learns to reward confidence, even when the model should express uncertainty.

This is the beginning of the reward hacking problem. The reward model is an imperfect proxy for what we actually want. It captures a noisy, sometimes distorted version of human preferences. And the R L stage will optimize against this proxy ruthlessly.

Now the third stage. Reinforcement learning with P P O.

You have an S F T model that follows instructions but has no continuous preference signal. You have a reward model that scores any response. The goal is to update the S F T model to produce responses that score higher under the reward model, while staying close enough to the S F T model that it does not degenerate.

P P O, proximal policy optimization, is the specific R L algorithm used. It was developed by Schulman and colleagues at OpenAI in twenty seventeen for robotics and game playing. Adapting it to language required rethinking some components, but the core algorithm is the same.

Here is how one training step works. Start with a batch of prompts. For each prompt, the current policy, which is the model being trained, generates a complete response by autoregressive sampling. The reward model scores each response. A value head, which is a small additional output layer attached to the policy model, predicts the expected reward for each prompt. The advantage for each response is the actual reward minus the value head's prediction. A positive advantage means the response was better than expected. A negative advantage means worse.

Now you update the policy. For each token in each generated response, you compute the probability ratio, the probability of that token under the new policy divided by the probability under the old policy. If the ratio is greater than one, the new policy is more likely to produce that token. If less than one, less likely. The P P O objective multiplies this ratio by the advantage and clips it. Specifically, the objective is the minimum of two terms. The unclipped ratio times advantage, and the clipped ratio times advantage, where the ratio is clipped to the range one minus epsilon to one plus epsilon. Epsilon is typically zero point two. The clipping prevents the policy from changing too much in a single step. If the ratio tries to go above one point two or below zero point eight, the gradient is zeroed out for that term. This is what makes P P O "proximal," it keeps each update close to the previous policy.

There is also a K L divergence penalty. The updated policy is penalized for drifting too far from the original S F T model, which serves as a reference policy. The K L term is computed per token, as the difference in log probabilities between the current policy and the frozen reference. This is critical. Without the K L penalty, the policy will exploit the reward model. It will find degenerate responses that score high under the reward model but are gibberish to a human. Verbose, repetitive, sycophantic, technically wrong in ways the reward model cannot detect. The K L penalty keeps the policy tethered to the S F T distribution, which is a safety net of coherent, well-formed language.

The balance between reward and K L penalty is controlled by a coefficient, often called beta. High beta means strong regularization, the policy stays close to the S F T model and does not deviate much. Low beta means the policy is free to explore and potentially find higher-reward regions, at the risk of degeneracy. In practice, beta is tuned to balance quality improvement against coherence maintenance, and the right value depends on how reliable the reward model is.

Let me give a concrete example of reward hacking, because it illustrates why the K L penalty and careful monitoring are essential. Early R L H F runs at OpenAI and Anthropic found that without sufficient regularization, models learned to produce very long, verbose responses filled with caveats and disclaimers. The reward model had been trained on human preferences where, all else equal, labelers tended to prefer more thorough responses. The policy learned to exploit this length bias ruthlessly. Responses would balloon to five or ten times the ideal length, stuffed with qualifications that added nothing. The reward model scored them highly. Humans would not. This is a canonical case of Goodhart's law, when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The reward model captures a lossy approximation of human preferences, and the R L process will find and exploit every gap between the approximation and the real thing.

The training dynamics of P P O also require careful monitoring. A typical R L H F training run tracks several metrics. The reward score should increase over training, indicating the model is learning to produce preferred responses. The K L divergence from the reference should increase but stay bounded, indicating the model is changing but not degenerating. The entropy of the policy's token distribution should decrease somewhat but not collapse, because a collapsed distribution means the model is producing repetitive, low-diversity outputs. If the reward goes up but the K L explodes, or if the entropy drops to near zero, training has gone wrong and should be stopped.

There is one more variant of R L H F worth mentioning, because it addresses the scalability problem of human labeling. Anthropic's Constitutional A I, or C A I, published in twenty twenty two, replaces much of the human preference labeling with A I feedback. Instead of having humans compare responses, you ask a language model to evaluate responses against a set of principles, a "constitution," that specifies what makes a response helpful, harmless, and honest. The A I generates preference labels, and those labels train the reward model.

This sounds circular, using an A I to train an A I. But it works for a specific reason. The evaluation task, deciding which of two responses is more helpful or less harmful, is easier than the generation task, producing a good response from scratch. A model can be a reliable judge even if it cannot reliably generate the better response itself. C A I dramatically reduces the cost of collecting preference data and allows you to iterate on the reward signal much faster. It also makes the preference criteria explicit and reproducible, since they are written down as principles rather than implicitly encoded in labeler guidelines that vary across individuals.

Let me be concrete about why this pipeline is operationally difficult. At each P P O step, you need four models in memory or at least accessible. The policy model being trained. The value head, usually a separate linear layer on top of the policy's last hidden state. The reward model, for scoring generated responses. And the reference model, the frozen S F T model, for computing the K L penalty. For a seventy billion parameter model, that is roughly four copies of seventy billion parameters. Even with quantization, mixed precision, and offloading, the memory footprint is enormous.

You also need online sampling. At each step, you generate complete responses from the current policy, which is slow, because generation is sequential and you need to produce full responses for an entire batch of prompts. And the hyperparameter space is large. Clip range, K L coefficient, learning rate, number of P P O epochs per batch, the value loss coefficient, the advantage normalization scheme, the generation temperature, the prompt distribution. Getting these wrong leads to training instability, reward hacking, or catastrophic forgetting of the base capabilities.

Despite all of this complexity, R L H F with P P O is what powered the original Chat G P T moment. The InstructG P T paper from early twenty twenty two showed that a one point three billion parameter model with R L H F was preferred by human evaluators over the one hundred and seventy-five billion parameter G P T three base model. Let that sink in. A model one hundred and thirty times smaller, fine-tuned with human preferences through S F T and P P O, beat the giant on human judgments of helpfulness and safety. That result was the proof of concept for the chatbot revolution.

The results were not just about overall preference. The paper showed specific improvements. R L H F reduced hallucination compared to S F T alone. It reduced toxic outputs. It improved the model's ability to follow complex instructions with multiple constraints. The reward model captured preference patterns that the S F T data alone could not express, and P P O optimized toward those patterns effectively.

Let me summarize the full arc. Pretraining gives you raw capability, a model that knows about the world but does not know it should help you. S F T gives you format and instruction following, turning the base model into something that responds to prompts in the right shape. The reward model captures human preferences as a continuous scoring function. P P O optimizes the model against those preferences while the K L penalty keeps it from degenerating. Together, these three stages turn a next-token predictor into a chatbot.

The limitations of this pipeline are what drove the next two episodes. P P O is complex, expensive, and operationally fragile. The reward model is an imperfect proxy that can be exploited. Human labeling is slow and expensive and does not scale. In the next episode, we are going to talk about D P O, Direct Preference Optimization, a twenty twenty three paper that asked whether you could skip the reward model entirely and the P P O entirely, and optimize preferences using a single supervised loss. The answer was yes, and it killed half the pipeline.
