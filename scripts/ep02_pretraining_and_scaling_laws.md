# Episode 2: Pretraining, scaling laws, and data

Welcome to episode two. Last time we walked through why the field threw out recurrence and bet everything on attention, and how that bet turned a sequential algorithm into a parallel one without losing modeling power. That was the architectural foundation. Today we look at the second foundation, which is the training recipe. The thing that turned a parallelizable architecture into a model that actually knows things about the world.

The core question is, why does "just predict the next token" produce something that can write code, reason about geography, debate philosophy, and pass the bar exam? It is not obvious. And along the way we are going to talk about the empirical laws that govern how big these models should be, how much data they need, and the twenty twenty two paper that revealed that for years, basically everyone in the field had been training their models wrong.

Let me start with the objective. The full name is autoregressive language modeling with cross-entropy loss. That is a mouthful. What it actually means is simple. You take a giant pile of text. You feed the model some prefix, like "the capital of France is." You ask, what is the next token? The correct answer is "Paris." You train the model to assign high probability to "Paris" and low probability to every other token in its vocabulary.

The way you measure "high probability" is the cross entropy, also called negative log likelihood. The model outputs a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary, which is typically tens of thousands of tokens. The loss is the negative log of the probability the model assigned to the actual next token. If the model said Paris had probability one, the loss is zero. If it said Paris had probability one in a thousand, the loss is about ten, in nats. If it said Paris had probability one in a million, the loss is closer to fourteen. Bigger loss for being more wrong. The shape of the log function is doing real work here. Being slightly less than perfect costs almost nothing. Being completely off costs a lot. That asymmetry is what makes this loss a useful learning signal across many orders of magnitude of probability.

There is a subtle but important point about this objective, and it is worth slowing down on. To assign high probability to the next token, the model has to actually know things. To predict that the token after "the capital of France is" is "Paris," the model has to have learned world geography. To predict that the next line of a function is a sensible return statement, the model has to have learned syntax and types and the function's intent. To predict the punchline of a joke, it has to have learned humor. Predicting the next token sounds shallow, but it is a Trojan horse for general world modeling. If you do it really well across enough text, you have implicitly built a model of the world that produced that text.

Ilya Sutskever, while he was at OpenAI, has framed this idea more provocatively. He argues that prediction is compression. To predict the next token well across a large corpus, your model has to find a compact representation of the rules that generated that corpus. The rules of physics, the structure of code, the patterns of human reasoning. And, in the limit, optimal compression is indistinguishable from intelligence. This is a philosophical argument and you do not have to fully buy it. But it is the argument behind why we should expect next-token prediction to scale to surprising capabilities. Compression of the Internet, taken seriously, is not a small ask.

A practical detail. During training, the model is given the entire correct prefix at every position, not its own predictions. Position five's input is the actual word at position four, not whatever the model would have said. This is called teacher forcing. It is what lets training be parallel, because, as we said in episode one, attention can compute every output position at once if it has all the inputs at once. At inference time, the model is sampling its own next token and feeding it back, which is sequential, but training does not have to be. This mismatch between training and inference, where the model only sees its own outputs at deploy time, is sometimes called exposure bias. It bothered people for a while. In practice, with enough scale, the issue mostly disappears, because the model becomes good enough that its own predictions resemble the training distribution closely. Most of the open problems with autoregressive sampling, like loops and degenerate repetition, get fixed by scale plus better decoding strategies, not by a fundamental change to the training objective.

Then there is the data side of the recipe. Take the entire Internet, do some filtering and deduplication, tokenize it, and train one big transformer to next-token predict on all of it. There is no labeling. No pairs of input and target. The text is its own supervision. This is what makes pretraining qualitatively different from the supervised learning paradigm that dominated machine learning before twenty seventeen. You do not need a human to draw a bounding box around every cat. You let the model predict the next byte of the Internet, and it figures things out.

A quick note on tokenization, because it is easy to gloss over but it shapes what the model can and cannot do. The model does not predict the next character or the next word. It predicts the next token, where tokens are subword units learned from the data using an algorithm called byte pair encoding, or B P E. B P E starts with individual characters and iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent pairs into new tokens. After enough merges, you get a vocabulary of, say, fifty thousand tokens. Common words like "the" and "and" become single tokens. Rare words get split into pieces. "Unbelievable" might become "un," "believ," "able." This is a compromise between character-level models, which have tiny vocabularies but need very long sequences, and word-level models, which have huge vocabularies and cannot handle new or misspelled words. Subword tokenization won because it balances vocabulary size, sequence length, and coverage of rare words. SentencePiece is a related library that implements B P E and other subword algorithms. Most modern models use some variant of B P E, and the vocabulary size, which typically ranges from thirty-two thousand to one hundred and twenty-eight thousand tokens, is a design choice that affects model capacity, sequence length, and multilingual performance.

OK, so that is the recipe. Big transformer. Teacher-forced cross entropy on huge text. The interesting question is, how should you spend your compute? If you have a million dollars worth of G P U time, do you train a small model for a long time, or a big model for a short time? Or something in between?

Before twenty twenty, the answer was vibes. You scaled up, you tried things, you wrote a paper. There was no theory. If you doubled the model size and the loss went down, great. You wrote it down and moved on.

In January twenty twenty, Jared Kaplan and several coauthors at OpenAI published a paper titled Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models. They had run a careful sweep across model sizes, dataset sizes, and compute budgets, and they found something remarkable. The validation loss on language modeling follows a smooth power law in three variables. The number of parameters in the model. The number of tokens in the training data. The total compute used.

A power law just means, plot it on log-log axes and you get a straight line. Loss versus parameters, straight line. Loss versus data, straight line. Loss versus compute, straight line. Across many orders of magnitude. From toy models with a few million parameters to G P T two scale models with over a billion. The line did not bend. There was no apparent saturation point on the horizon. The implication was that you could pour another factor of ten compute into the system and confidently predict where the loss would land, before the run started.

This was a big deal because it meant you could now forecast performance. You could draw a straight line on a chart and predict, with surprising accuracy, what loss a model ten times bigger would achieve, before you trained it. This was unheard of in deep learning. Usually you train and pray. Now you could plan.

Kaplan and the OpenAI team also gave a prescription for compute optimal training. If you have a fixed compute budget and you want minimum loss, how do you split that compute between making the model bigger and training it longer? Their answer was, mostly grow the model. Specifically, when you scale compute by a factor of ten, you should scale model size by roughly a factor of five and the dataset by only a factor of two. The model should grow much faster than the dataset.

This prescription shaped the next two and a half years of large language model development. G P T three, released later in twenty twenty, has one hundred and seventy five billion parameters and was trained on around three hundred billion tokens. The ratio of training tokens to parameters is less than two. Tiny. In Kaplan's framework, that was fine. The recipe said, make the model big, train it briefly, that is compute optimal. So that is what people did.

Then in March twenty twenty two, a team at DeepMind led by Jordan Hoffmann published a paper called Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models. The community calls it the Chinchilla paper, after the model they trained to demonstrate the result. And the paper said, politely but unmistakably, that the original scaling laws had gotten the prescription wrong.

Their methodology was patient. They trained over four hundred models, ranging in size from seventy million to over sixteen billion parameters, on dataset sizes ranging from five billion to five hundred billion tokens. They were careful about the experimental design. They controlled for compute and varied the way that compute was spent across model size and data. And they fit a more careful curve.

The Chinchilla finding. For a fixed compute budget, the optimal way to spend it is to grow model size and training data roughly equally. Each doubling of compute should be split between two times more parameters and two times more tokens, give or take. Not Kaplan's lopsided split, but something close to balanced.

There is a simple practical takeaway from the Chinchilla curves. The compute-optimal ratio between training tokens and model parameters is approximately twenty. To optimally train a one billion parameter model, you should give it about twenty billion tokens. To optimally train a seventy billion parameter model, you should train it on around one point four trillion tokens. This twenty-tokens-per-parameter rule of thumb became the headline number people quoted from the paper.

Now look at G P T three under this lens. One hundred and seventy five billion parameters. Three hundred billion tokens. The Chinchilla-optimal training set for a one hundred and seventy five billion parameter model would be around three and a half trillion tokens, more than ten times what G P T three actually got. G P T three was massively undertrained. The model was way too big for the data it was trained on. By spending more compute on data and less on parameters, DeepMind argued, you could get a strictly better model for the same total compute.

To prove the point, they trained Chinchilla itself. Seventy billion parameters, one point four trillion tokens. Total compute about the same as Gopher, an earlier DeepMind model that was two hundred and eighty billion parameters trained on about three hundred billion tokens. Chinchilla, with one quarter of the parameters of Gopher, beat Gopher across nearly every benchmark. Same compute. Better results. Smaller model.

The implication was sweeping. Most of the giant models trained in twenty twenty and twenty twenty one, including G P T three, were probably not as good as smaller models trained on more data would have been at the same compute. The whole field had been spending its compute wrong.

Why did Kaplan get this wrong? The most cited methodological reason is that the original paper used a learning rate schedule that did not adapt to the total training duration. As you change the dataset size, the optimal learning rate schedule changes too, and Kaplan's experiments did not capture that effect cleanly. There were a few other details. The point is, Chinchilla was a more careful sweep with a more honest experimental setup. And the answer it gave was different.

Now here is the twist that turns this from a clean academic story into something more interesting. After Chinchilla, the field briefly tried to actually follow the Chinchilla recipe. And then Meta released Llama in February twenty twenty three.

Llama trained a seven billion parameter model on one trillion tokens. The Chinchilla-optimal data for a seven billion parameter model is roughly one hundred and forty billion tokens. Llama trained on seven times more than that. Way past compute-optimal, in Chinchilla terms.

Why? Because Chinchilla was answering one specific question. What is the cheapest way to reach a certain loss, accounting only for training compute? But that is not actually what you care about if you are deploying a model to a billion users. Once a model is trained, you serve it billions of times. Training cost is paid once. Inference cost is paid forever. If a smaller model can be trained to the same quality by giving it more data, then for the same eventual quality you have a smaller model to serve. Smaller model means cheaper inference, less memory, faster latency. And those savings, summed over the lifetime of the deployed model, dwarf the extra training compute it took to overtrain.

So the post-Chinchilla recipe is, train past Chinchilla optimal. Smaller models, more data, more training. Llama three, released in twenty twenty four, takes this much further. The eight billion parameter model is trained on fifteen trillion tokens. That is a tokens-to-parameters ratio of around eighteen hundred. Almost a hundred times the Chinchilla-optimal ratio. And it works. Llama three eight billion is competitive with much larger models from a year earlier.

So this is the current era. Compute-optimal scaling laws gave us a beautiful theory in twenty twenty two, and the industry promptly walked away from it because the deployment economics make over-training the right call. Both things are true at once. Chinchilla is correct about training cost. Llama is correct about total cost of ownership.

Where does that leave us. A few things.

One, scaling laws as a research program were maybe the most important conceptual shift to happen in deep learning between twenty twenty and twenty twenty three. Not because the prescriptions were always right, but because they shifted the field from alchemy to forecasting. You can now reason about what a hypothetical model would do before you train it. That capability did not exist before.

Two, we have moved past the era where parameter count was the main lever. The frontier models of twenty twenty five are not dramatically larger than G P T four was. What changed is data. Not just more data, but better data. The raw Internet is full of duplicates, spam, machine-generated slop, toxic content, and low-quality text. Modern pretraining pipelines invest enormous effort in data curation. Deduplication, both exact and fuzzy, removes redundant documents that would otherwise waste training compute on memorization rather than learning. Perplexity filtering uses a small reference model to score documents and removes those that look like gibberish, auto-generated text, or boilerplate. Toxicity and P I I filters remove content that would teach the model harmful or private behavior. Domain classification tags each document, web text, books, code, scientific papers, social media, so you can control the mix.

That mix is itself a critical design choice. Llama three's pretraining data was roughly fifty percent web text, twenty-five percent code, and the remainder split across books, scientific text, and curated high-quality sources. The code fraction is much higher than you might expect for a general-purpose language model, but training on code turns out to improve reasoning and instruction following even on non-code tasks. The mixture weights are tuned empirically, and different mixes produce models with different strengths.

There is also curriculum design, the order in which data is presented during training. Some teams train on lower-quality web data early and transition to higher-quality curated data later in training, a practice sometimes called annealing. The intuition is that early training benefits from breadth and diversity, while late training benefits from polish and quality. Llama three used a multi-stage data curriculum with increasing quality, and ablation studies showed it mattered.

The broader point is that the data side of the recipe became where most of the marginal gains came from after twenty twenty three. Architecture is settled. Scale is expensive. Data quality and curation is where a careful team can still find large improvements.

Three, this in turn raises the question of when we run out of high-quality text. Estimates differ, but you can find serious researchers arguing we have already trained on most of the genuinely useful public Internet. Which is part of why synthetic data, where a strong model generates training data for the next generation, has become such a big deal. Phi from Microsoft is the well-known example. We may end up in a world where you bootstrap your data quality using model-generated content rather than waiting for humans to write more of it.

Four, scaling laws also work in directions other than next-token loss. There are scaling laws for downstream task performance, for emergent capabilities, for chain-of-thought reasoning, for fine-tuning. Most of them follow similar power-law shapes. This is the deeper observation under the surface. The capabilities of these systems are not magic. They are predictable. Mostly.

Five, the next-token prediction objective itself is evolving. DeepSeek V three, released in late twenty twenty four, trains with multi-token prediction. Instead of predicting just the next token at each position, the model predicts the next two, three, or four tokens simultaneously, using additional prediction heads. Each extra head shares the same transformer backbone but has its own small output layer that predicts a different future position. The training loss is the sum of the losses across all prediction heads.

Why does this help? It provides a richer training signal per sequence. Standard next-token prediction gives one gradient signal per position. Multi-token prediction gives N signals per position, each capturing a different scale of future context. Predicting two tokens ahead requires understanding slightly longer-range dependencies than predicting one token ahead. The model learns faster per training token, which translates to better data efficiency. Meta's research on multi-token prediction, published in twenty twenty four, showed consistent improvements in code generation benchmarks, where longer-range planning is particularly important.

There is a practical bonus. A model trained with multi-token prediction naturally produces the draft predictions needed for speculative decoding, the inference speedup technique we will cover in episode four. The extra prediction heads can serve as a built-in draft model, proposing multiple future tokens that the main model verifies in a single forward pass. You get both better training and faster inference from the same architectural change.

Six, and this is where the story is going. Because scaling pretrained models hits diminishing returns, the field has been adding new dimensions of compute. Test-time compute, where the model thinks longer before answering. Reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards, where the model gets better through interaction. We will get to all of that in later episodes. But the foundation is what you have to know first. Pretraining is the substrate. Everything else builds on top of a model that has already eaten the Internet.

If I had to compress this episode to one paragraph. The pretraining recipe is autoregressive next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss on a giant text corpus. It works because predicting the next token is a Trojan horse for world modeling. Scaling laws made the relationship between compute, data, parameters, and loss into a smooth, forecastable curve. Chinchilla, in twenty twenty two, showed that the optimal compute split is much more data-heavy than originally thought. And then the industry collectively decided to over-train anyway, because inference economics changed the math. The recipe today is, take a smaller model than the original scaling laws suggested, train it on much more data than even Chinchilla recommended, and serve it cheaply for years.
