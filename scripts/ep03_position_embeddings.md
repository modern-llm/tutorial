# Episode 3: Position embeddings, from sinusoidal to RoPE

Welcome to episode three. At the end of episode one, I mentioned something that is easy to overlook. The attention mechanism, by itself, has no notion of order. If you shuffle every token in a sentence and run it through a standard attention layer, the output for each token changes, but only because the values it averages over are different, not because the layer knows anything about position. Attention treats its input as an unordered set. That is a feature for parallelism, but it is a problem for language, because word order matters. "The dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" contain the same tokens.

So you have to inject position information somehow. And the way you do it turns out to matter enormously, especially once you start trying to handle long sequences. Today we are going to walk through how the field went from sinusoidal position encodings, to learned position embeddings, to rotary position embeddings, which is what essentially every modern L L M uses. The arc is prior approach, what changed, why it won.

The original transformer paper in twenty seventeen used sinusoidal position encodings. The idea was elegant. For each position in the sequence, you construct a vector of the same dimension as the token embedding, using sine and cosine functions at different frequencies. Think of it this way. The embedding dimension is, say, five hundred and twelve. You pair up the dimensions, giving you two hundred and fifty-six pairs. Each pair gets assigned a different frequency. The first pair oscillates very slowly, completing one full cycle over thousands of positions. The last pair oscillates very quickly, completing a cycle every few positions. For a given position, you compute the sine at that position's angle for the first element of the pair, and the cosine for the second element. Stack all of these up across all two hundred and fifty-six pairs, and you get a unique vector for each position.

Why sines and cosines? The authors gave a specific reason. For any fixed offset k, there exists a linear transformation that maps the encoding at position t to the encoding at position t plus k. This comes from the trigonometric addition formulas. Sine of a plus b equals sine a cosine b plus cosine a sine b. So if you have the sine and cosine values at position t, you can compute the values at position t plus k by applying a rotation matrix that depends only on k, not on t. In other words, relative position relationships are encoded as linear operations. The model could, in principle, learn to attend to "three positions ago" by learning a simple linear function of the position vectors. That was the theory.

In practice, sinusoidal encodings worked fine for the sequence lengths people cared about in twenty seventeen. Translation sentences were rarely longer than a few hundred tokens. But there was a fundamental limitation. The model was trained on sequences up to some maximum length, say five hundred and twelve tokens. What happens at position five hundred and thirteen? The sine and cosine values are well-defined mathematically, but the model has never seen them during training. The learned weights that consume those position vectors were tuned for a specific range of input patterns. Going outside that range is extrapolation, and the model's behavior becomes unpredictable. In practice, it produced garbage.

There is a subtler issue too. Sinusoidal encodings are added to the token embeddings at the input, before the first attention layer. By the time the signal reaches layer twelve or layer twenty, it has been through many nonlinear transformations. The clean geometric properties of the sine-cosine vectors, the linear relative-position structure, get mangled. The model has to rediscover position information from the residual stream, where it has been mixed with content information in complicated ways.

The next step was learned position embeddings. Instead of computing position vectors from a formula, just make them trainable parameters. Have a lookup table with one learned vector per position, from position zero to position max length minus one. Add the learned vector to the token embedding, same as before. This is what G P T two and G P T three used. B E R T used it. Most models in the twenty nineteen to twenty twenty one era used it.

Learned embeddings had one advantage. The model could learn whatever position representation worked best for the data, rather than being locked into sinusoids. If the task-optimal position encoding is not sinusoidal, learned embeddings can find it. And in practice they performed slightly better than sinusoidal encodings on the benchmarks of the day.

But they had the same hard ceiling. The lookup table has a fixed number of entries. If your table goes up to position one thousand and twenty four, position one thousand and twenty five does not exist. There is no embedding for it. You can try to extrapolate, by interpolating between nearby learned vectors, but it does not work well. The learned vectors have no guaranteed smooth structure. Adjacent positions might have very different vectors. The model produces incoherent output beyond its trained context window.

Both sinusoidal and learned approaches also share a subtler issue. They encode absolute position. Token five gets the embedding for position five, always, regardless of context. But what matters for language is usually relative position. Whether one word is three positions before another, not whether it sits at position forty two or position eight hundred and twelve. Absolute position information leaks into every attention computation, and the model has to learn to ignore the absolute part and extract the relative part. It can do this, sort of, but it is asking the model to do unnecessary work. And it means the model's understanding of "three tokens apart" at position fifty may be different from its understanding of "three tokens apart" at position five hundred, because the absolute position vectors are different even though the relationship is the same.

So by twenty twenty one, the wish list was clear. An ideal position encoding would capture relative positions directly. It would not have a hard length limit. It would be computationally cheap. And it would inject position information at the right place in the computation, in the attention scores rather than in the input embeddings.

A L i B i, proposed by Press, Smith, and Lewis in twenty twenty one, took one approach. Instead of modifying the embeddings at all, A L i B i adds a linear bias to the attention scores. The bias is proportional to the distance between the query and key positions. Positions that are far apart get penalized, positions that are close get a boost. Each attention head uses a different slope, so some heads focus locally and others attend more broadly. A L i B i is simple, cheap, and extrapolates well to longer sequences than seen during training. Its main limitation is expressiveness. The bias is a single scalar per distance value per head. It can only express monotonically decreasing attention with distance, which is a strong assumption about what the model should attend to.

The approach that won, and that is now used by essentially every major L L M, is R o P E. Rotary Position Embedding. Proposed by Su and colleagues in twenty twenty one, in a paper called RoFormer.

Here is the core idea of R o P E, and I want to be precise because this shows up everywhere from here on. Instead of adding a position vector to the token embedding, R o P E rotates the query and key vectors by an angle that depends on position. The value vectors are left alone. Only queries and keys get rotated. And crucially, the rotation happens after the linear projections that produce Q and K, not before. This means position information is injected directly into the attention score computation, not mixed into the content representation at the input.

Let me unpack what rotation means here, because it is worth understanding the geometry. Take the query vector for a token at position m. It has d dimensions. Pair up those dimensions, two at a time. Dimensions zero and one form a pair. Dimensions two and three form another pair. And so on, giving you d over two pairs. For each pair, apply a two-dimensional rotation by an angle that is m times some frequency.

A two-dimensional rotation is the simplest possible geometric operation in a plane. Take a point at coordinates x, y. Rotate it by angle phi. The new coordinates are x cosine phi minus y sine phi, and x sine phi plus y cosine phi. That is it. The point stays at the same distance from the origin, it just moves around the circle.

Different pairs of dimensions get different frequencies. The frequency for pair i is one over theta to the power of two i over d, where theta is the base parameter, defaulting to ten thousand. This means the first pair has a frequency near one, it rotates quickly. The last pair has a very low frequency, it rotates very slowly. This is the same multi-scale idea as the original sinusoidal encoding, but applied as rotations to Q and K rather than additions to the input.

Now, when you compute the dot product of the rotated query at position m with the rotated key at position n, something elegant happens. The rotation angles partially cancel. Consider a single pair of dimensions. The query pair has been rotated by angle m times theta i. The key pair has been rotated by angle n times theta i. The dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on the angle between them, which is m minus n times theta i. This is a fundamental property of rotations. If I hand you two vectors and tell you they have been rotated, you can compute their relative angle without knowing the absolute angle of either one.

This means the attention score between positions m and n depends only on m minus n. Relative position, baked into the geometry, without any extra parameters or lookup tables.

The frequency base theta, which defaults to ten thousand in the original paper, controls the wavelength of the rotations. At theta equals ten thousand, the slowest-rotating pair completes one full cycle over about sixty thousand positions. The fastest-rotating pair completes a cycle every six or seven positions. Together they give the model a multi-scale sense of distance. Fast-rotating pairs distinguish nearby tokens, positions one, two, three. Slow-rotating pairs distinguish far-apart tokens, positions one hundred versus one thousand. The model can use different pairs for different kinds of positional reasoning.

Why does R o P E extrapolate better than learned embeddings? Because there is no lookup table. The rotation at position ten thousand is well-defined, it is just a bigger angle. The mathematical structure is continuous. The dot products at long range still follow the same relative-position formula. In practice, R o P E does not extrapolate perfectly out of the box. The issue is that during training, the model only sees a limited range of angles. The high-frequency pairs, which rotate fast, will have gone through many full cycles even within the training context. So the model has seen all possible angles for those pairs, and they generalize fine. But the low-frequency pairs may not have completed a full cycle during training. At inference on longer sequences, those pairs produce angles the model has never seen, and the attention patterns degrade.

The key insight is that this degradation is smooth, not catastrophic. The dot products change continuously as position changes. There is no cliff edge where the model suddenly breaks. And this smooth degradation is what makes post-hoc context extension possible. You can adjust the frequencies, essentially slowing down the rotations, to make longer sequences map back to familiar angle ranges. We will cover this in detail in episode six, when we talk about N T K scaling and Y a R N.

There is one more technical detail about R o P E that connects to episode one. Remember that attention uses three projections, query, key, and value. R o P E only modifies query and key. The value vectors carry no position information at all. This is deliberate and important. The attention pattern, which tokens attend to which, should be position-aware. That is what Q and K determine. But the content that gets passed through, the value, does not need to know where it came from. It just needs to carry the right information. This clean separation between "where to look" and "what to carry" means that the value pathway is purely about content, and the Q K pathway handles position. This separation composes beautifully with other techniques. Grouped-query attention, which shares K V heads across query groups, works because the value does not need position-specific diversity. K V cache compression works because you can manipulate cached keys and values without worrying about position information in the values.

Let me also address a subtlety about how R o P E interacts with the attention computation at a linear algebra level. In standard attention without R o P E, the attention score for a pair of positions is q transpose times k, where q and k are vectors. With R o P E, it is q transpose times R of m minus n times k, where R is a block-diagonal rotation matrix. But because R is orthogonal, meaning R transpose equals R inverse, this is the same as R of m transpose times q, dotted with R of n transpose times k. In other words, you can either rotate both q and k by their respective positions and dot them, or you can apply a single relative rotation to one of them. In practice, the first form is what implementations use, because you can rotate q and k once and then compute all the dot products.

Let me summarize why R o P E won, compared to the alternatives. Versus sinusoidal encodings, R o P E encodes relative position directly in the attention computation, rather than hoping the model figures it out from absolute position vectors added at the input. Versus learned embeddings, R o P E has no fixed length limit and extrapolates to unseen positions with smooth degradation. Versus A L i B i, R o P E is far more expressive. A L i B i uses a single scalar bias per distance per head. R o P E encodes position information across every dimension pair in the query and key vectors, giving the model a much richer and more flexible position signal. And R o P E's computational cost is negligible, just some extra sines, cosines, and multiplications on Q and K, which are small compared to the attention matrix computation.

The adoption story is clear. Llama, Llama two, Llama three, Mistral, Mixtral, Falcon, Qwen, DeepSeek, Gemma. R o P E. It is not quite universal, some models experiment with variations. But it is the overwhelming default for every significant open-weight model released after twenty twenty two.

One last practical point. R o P E plays nicely with inference optimization. Because the rotation is applied to queries and keys, and not to values, the K V cache stores rotated keys. When a new query comes in at position t, you rotate it by t and dot-product it against the cached keys, which were already rotated by their respective positions when they entered the cache. The relative-position math just works. No recomputation needed. This is a small detail that matters a lot when you are serving millions of requests and every microsecond in the attention computation counts.
