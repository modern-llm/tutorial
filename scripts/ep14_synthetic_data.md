# Episode 14: Synthetic data and the data flywheel

Welcome to episode fourteen. In episode two, we talked about the pretraining recipe and how Chinchilla showed that data matters as much as model size. We also touched on a looming problem. We might be running out of high-quality human-generated text. Estimates vary, but several researchers have argued that by twenty twenty five, the major labs have already trained on most of the genuinely useful public Internet. The total stock of high-quality English text is finite, somewhere in the low trillions of tokens, and frontier models already train on most of it.

This creates a data bottleneck. Scaling laws say more data should give better models. But where does the data come from if you have already used most of what humans wrote? The answer, increasingly, is from the models themselves. This is synthetic data. A strong model generates training data for the next generation of models, or for fine-tuning specialized variants. It is one of the most important and most controversial trends in modern L L M development.

Let me start with the earliest and cleanest success story. Self-Instruct, published by Wang and colleagues in late twenty twenty two. The problem it solved was simple. To fine-tune a base model to follow instructions, as we discussed in episode nine on R L H F, you need instruction-response pairs. Collecting these from humans is expensive and slow. Self-Instruct generates them from the model itself.

The recipe works like this. Start with a small seed set of human-written instruction-response pairs, around one hundred and seventy-five examples. Prompt a strong language model, in the original paper this was G P T three, to generate new instructions by analogy with the seed set. Then prompt the model again to generate responses to those new instructions. Filter out low-quality generations using simple heuristics, too short, too similar to existing instructions, malformed. The result is a large dataset of instruction-response pairs generated entirely by the model, seeded by a tiny amount of human data.

Stanford's Alpaca, in early twenty twenty three, applied this at scale. They used G P T three point five to generate fifty-two thousand instruction-response pairs and fine-tuned Llama seven B on them. The resulting model was surprisingly capable for its size and cost. It demonstrated that the instruction-following capability unlocked by S F T did not require expensive human-written data. Model-generated data was sufficient.

This was the opening move, but the real proof of concept for synthetic data as a training strategy came from Microsoft's Phi series.

Phi one, released in late twenty twenty three, trained a one point three billion parameter model that outperformed models five to ten times its size on code benchmarks. The key was not architecture or training scale. It was data quality. The training data was a mix of filtered web data and synthetic "textbook quality" data generated by G P T three point five. The synthetic component consisted of exercises, explanations, and worked examples in programming, structured like a well-written textbook rather than like random code scraped from GitHub.

Phi two extended this to two point seven billion parameters, with more synthetic data covering reasoning, math, and science. Phi three, at three point eight billion parameters, used an even larger synthetic corpus and competitive post-training. The Phi series demonstrated a specific claim. For a fixed compute budget, spending that compute on curating and generating high-quality data, and then training a small model on it, can beat spending the same compute on training a larger model on lower-quality data.

This is a different angle on the Chinchilla insight from episode two. Chinchilla said, at a given compute budget, balance model size and data quantity. Phi says, at a given compute budget, invest in data quality, and you can get away with a much smaller model. The two are not contradictory. They are complementary. Quality and quantity are both levers, and quality may have a higher ceiling.

There are several flavors of synthetic data that are worth distinguishing because they have different characteristics.

Synthetic pretraining data. Model-generated text used to augment or replace web-scraped data for pretraining. This is what Phi uses. The quality bar is high because pretraining data shapes the model's foundational knowledge.

Synthetic instruction data. Model-generated instruction-response pairs used for S F T. This is what Self-Instruct and Alpaca use. The quality bar is lower because S F T data mainly needs to demonstrate the correct format and a reasonable range of tasks.

Synthetic preference data. Model-generated comparisons used for D P O or reward model training. You can generate two responses to the same prompt from different models, or from the same model at different temperatures, and use a strong model to judge which is better. Constitutional A I, which we covered in episode nine, is a version of this.

Synthetic reasoning traces. Long chain-of-thought solutions generated by a reasoning model, used to distill reasoning capability into smaller models. This is what DeepSeek R one Distill uses, as we covered in episode twelve. The strong reasoning model generates thousands of high-quality reasoning traces, and a smaller model is S F T trained on them.

Rejection sampling. Generate many candidate responses to each prompt, score them with a reward model or verifiable checker, and keep only the best. The resulting dataset is model-generated but filtered to high quality. This is used extensively in the post-training of Llama three and other frontier models. It is synthetic data with a quality filter.

Each of these has different risk profiles. Synthetic pretraining data carries the highest risk because errors in foundational knowledge propagate through everything the model does. Synthetic instruction data is lower risk because the format matters more than the content. Synthetic reasoning traces are medium risk because the reasoning style is what transfers, not the specific answers, and the answers can be verified.

Now let me address the central concern with synthetic data. Model collapse.

Model collapse, described by Shumailov and colleagues in twenty twenty three, is the phenomenon where training a model on data generated by a previous generation of models leads to progressive quality degradation. Each generation is a slightly lossy copy of the previous one. Over multiple generations, rare but important features of the original data distribution get lost. The distribution narrows. The tails shrink. Diversity decreases. After enough generations, the model produces bland, repetitive, generic output that lacks the richness and variety of the original human-generated data.

The intuition is simple. A model trained on Internet text learns to produce text that is similar to Internet text, but not identical. It captures the most common patterns well but slightly underrepresents rare patterns. If you train the next model on this model's output, the rare patterns are underrepresented twice. After ten generations, the rare patterns are effectively gone.

This is a real phenomenon. It has been demonstrated empirically. But it is also more nuanced than the "A I eating its own tail" narrative suggests.

First, model collapse is primarily a problem when synthetic data completely replaces human data. If you mix synthetic data with a core of real human-generated data, the real data anchors the distribution and prevents the collapse. Most practical systems maintain a substantial fraction of real data in their training mix. Llama three, for instance, uses synthetic data as a supplement, not a replacement.

Second, the quality of the synthetic data matters more than its origin. Carefully generated, filtered, and curated synthetic data can be higher quality than random web scraping, even though it comes from a model. The Phi results demonstrate this. The issue is not "model-generated versus human-generated." It is "high-quality versus low-quality."

Third, verification is a powerful defense against collapse. If you can verify the correctness of synthetic data, as with math and code, you can filter out the degraded samples and keep only the good ones. This is why synthetic data works so well for reasoning, because you can check the answers, and less reliably for open-ended text, because there is no automatic quality check.

Let me describe the data flywheel pattern, because this is the direction the field is heading. A strong model generates data. That data trains a better model. The better model generates better data. The cycle repeats. Each iteration, the data quality and the model quality improve together.

DeepSeek R one is an example of this flywheel in action. Start with a strong base model. Train it with R L on verifiable tasks to produce a reasoning model. Use the reasoning model to generate high-quality reasoning traces. Distill those traces into smaller models via S F T. Use the smaller models to generate data for the next round. Each cycle, the reasoning traces get better because the generating model is better.

The question is whether this flywheel has diminishing returns or can sustain indefinite improvement. The optimistic view is that each generation unlocks new capabilities that generate novel data that the previous generation could not produce. The pessimistic view is that without genuinely new information from the real world, the model will plateau, recycling its own knowledge without gaining new understanding. The truth is probably somewhere in between, and it likely depends on the domain. For math and code, where problems can be generated combinatorially and solutions verified, the flywheel may have a lot of runway. For knowledge about the physical world, which requires observation and not just computation, the flywheel is limited by the information already encoded in the base model.

Let me make the data flywheel concrete with two examples.

Llama three's post-training pipeline uses rejection sampling extensively. For each prompt in their S F T dataset, they generate multiple candidate responses from the current best model. Each response is scored by a reward model. Only the highest-scoring responses are kept. The resulting dataset is synthetic, generated by a model, but it is higher quality than the original human-written S F T data because the reward model filters out mediocre responses. Meta reported that iterating this process, training a new model on the filtered data, then using that model to generate a new round of candidates, produced consistent improvements over multiple rounds. Each round, the generating model is better, the candidates are better, the filtered dataset is better, and the next model is better.

DeepSeek's R one distillation pipeline is an even cleaner example. Start with the R one reasoning model, which was trained with G R P O on verifiable math and code tasks. Generate reasoning traces from R one on a diverse problem set. Use those traces as S F T data for smaller models, one point five billion, seven billion, fourteen billion, and seventy billion parameters. The distilled models outperform same-size models trained with their own R L, because R one's traces are higher quality than what a small model could discover through exploration. Then use the distilled models to generate data for the next iteration. Each cycle, the traces get better because the generating model is better.

The practical lesson from both examples is that the flywheel works when you have a quality filter. Rejection sampling uses a reward model as the filter. Distillation uses verifiable correctness as the filter. Without a filter, you get model collapse. With a filter, you get a virtuous cycle.

The operational version of this is a data curation pipeline, not a single prompt. Generate many candidates. Deduplicate aggressively. Score them with a reward model, a verifier, a unit test, or a stronger model judge. Keep the best. Mix them with real human data so the distribution stays anchored. Train a new model. Then repeat. Synthetic data is powerful when it is the raw material for a filter-and-train loop. It is dangerous when it is treated as free replacement text.

To close this episode differently than the others. The story of synthetic data is really a story about whether intelligence can bootstrap itself. Can a model teach the next model to be smarter? The evidence so far says yes, within domains where quality can be measured, and with real limits that we do not yet fully understand. The data flywheel is one of the current trajectories of the field, but it depends on verification, curation, and honest evaluation.

In the next episode, we are going to talk about evaluation. How do we actually measure whether a model is any good? Benchmarks, leaderboards, human preference ratings, and the hard problem of contamination. When models might have trained on the test set, how do you trust the score?
