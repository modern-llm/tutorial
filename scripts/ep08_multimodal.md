# Episode 8: Multimodal models

Welcome to episode eight. Everything we have discussed so far in this series has been about text. Predicting the next token, encoding positions in sequences of words, compressing the K V cache for token sequences, extending context, and adapting text models cheaply. But the frontier models of twenty twenty five, G P T four, Gemini, Claude, Llama three point two, are not text-only. They see images. Some process video and audio. They can look at a photograph and describe what is in it, read a chart and answer questions about the data, examine a screenshot of code and spot the bug, or take a picture of a whiteboard and convert the diagram to structured text.

Today we are going to talk about how language models gained vision. The arc follows the same pattern as the rest of this series. Prior approach, what changed, why it won.

The prior approach, before roughly twenty twenty one, was to train separate models for vision and language and then combine them in task-specific ways. You would have a convolutional neural network, a ResNet or similar, trained on ImageNet for image classification. And you would have a language model, a transformer, trained on text. To do something like image captioning, you would use the C N N as a feature extractor, take its output, and feed it into the language model as a conditioning signal. Each task, captioning, visual question answering, image retrieval, had its own architecture and its own training pipeline. Nothing was shared.

The first major shift was C L I P, Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining, published by OpenAI in January twenty twenty one. C L I P trains two encoders, one for images and one for text, to produce embeddings in a shared vector space. The training data is four hundred million image-text pairs scraped from the Internet. The training objective is contrastive. For a batch of image-text pairs, the model learns to maximize the similarity between matching pairs and minimize the similarity between non-matching pairs. If you have a batch of one thousand images and one thousand captions, the model learns that image one should be close to caption one in embedding space, and far from captions two through one thousand.

After training, C L I P can do zero-shot image classification. To classify an image, you encode the image and encode a set of text descriptions like "a photo of a cat" and "a photo of a dog." Whichever text embedding is closest to the image embedding is the classification. No task-specific training needed. C L I P also became the standard image encoder for downstream multimodal systems, because its image embeddings are already aligned with language.

But C L I P is not a generative model. It maps images and text to a shared embedding space, which is useful for retrieval and classification, but it cannot generate text descriptions, answer questions about images, or have conversations about visual content. For that, you need to connect a vision encoder to a language model.

The simplest architecture for this, and the one that won, is what I will call the late-fusion approach. Take a pretrained vision encoder, typically a Vision Transformer or V i T, often initialized from C L I P. Take a pretrained language model. Connect them with a projection layer that maps the vision encoder's output embeddings into the language model's input space. Then fine-tune the combined system on image-text tasks.

Let me be more specific about how this works mechanically. The V i T divides the image into fixed-size patches, typically fourteen by fourteen or sixteen by sixteen pixels. Each patch is linearly projected into an embedding vector, just like a token embedding in a text transformer. These patch embeddings are processed by the V i T's transformer layers, producing a sequence of output vectors, one per patch. For a two hundred and twenty-four by two hundred and twenty-four pixel image with fourteen by fourteen patches, you get two hundred and fifty-six patch embeddings.

The projection layer, which is sometimes a single linear layer and sometimes a small M L P, maps these patch embeddings from the V i T's dimension to the language model's dimension. The projected patch embeddings are then treated as if they were token embeddings and prepended to the text token sequence. From the language model's perspective, the image is just a sequence of "visual tokens" that appear before the text tokens. The language model's attention mechanism attends to these visual tokens the same way it attends to text tokens. No architectural change to the language model is needed. It processes the combined visual and text token sequence with its existing attention layers.

LLaVA, Large Language and Visual Assistant, published by Liu and colleagues in late twenty twenty three, demonstrated that this simple approach works remarkably well. LLaVA one point five uses a C L I P V i T as the vision encoder, a two-layer M L P as the projection, and Vicuna, a fine-tuned Llama model, as the language model. The total amount of multimodal training data is around six hundred thousand image-text instruction examples. The training is done in two stages. First, freeze the language model and the vision encoder, and train only the projection M L P on image-caption pairs. This teaches the projection to map visual features into the language model's embedding space. Second, unfreeze the language model and fine-tune the language model and projection together on visual instruction data, like "describe this image" or "what color is the car in this photo?"

The result is a model that can have conversations about images, answer detailed questions about visual content, read text in images, and reason about spatial relationships. And the recipe is simple enough that it was reproduced by dozens of research groups within months.

Why does late fusion work so well? The key insight is that both the vision encoder and the language model are already very capable, trained on billions of images and trillions of tokens respectively. The projection layer does not need to do heavy lifting. It just needs to translate between two already-rich representation spaces. The visual tokens arrive in the language model's space with enough information for the language model to reason about them using its existing capabilities. The language model does not need to learn vision from scratch. It already knows how to reason, follow instructions, and generate text. It just needs visual inputs in a format it can attend to.

This stands in contrast to the early fusion approach, where you would train a single model from scratch on interleaved image and text data. Early fusion has the potential to learn deeper cross-modal interactions, but it requires much more data and compute, and you lose the benefit of leveraging separately pretrained components. Flamingo, from DeepMind in twenty twenty two, took a middle path. It used a frozen pretrained vision encoder and a frozen pretrained language model, but connected them with gated cross-attention layers inserted between the language model's existing layers. These cross-attention layers allow the language model to attend to visual features at multiple depths, not just at the input. This is more expressive than LLaVA's approach but also more complex and harder to train.

In practice, the simple late-fusion approach, where visual tokens are prepended to the text and processed by the existing attention layers, became the dominant paradigm. LLaVA, Intern V L, Qwen-VL, and many others use this pattern. The frontier models, G P T four V, Gemini, and Claude, have not disclosed their exact architectures, but the observable behavior is consistent with late fusion, visual tokens processed alongside text tokens in a unified transformer.

There are several important technical details worth covering.

Resolution and token count. Higher resolution images produce more patches and therefore more visual tokens. A two hundred and twenty-four pixel image with sixteen pixel patches gives one hundred and ninety-six visual tokens. A four hundred and forty-eight pixel image gives seven hundred and eighty-four. A high-resolution photograph at seven hundred and sixty-eight pixels gives over two thousand visual tokens. These visual tokens consume context window space and add to the K V cache, connecting directly to the efficiency concerns from episode four. Some models use dynamic resolution, processing the image at multiple scales or only processing the relevant regions at high resolution to control the token count.

Visual grounding. The patch-based representation naturally encodes spatial information. The model can learn to associate specific patches with specific regions of the image. This enables tasks like pointing, where the model identifies the location of an object by referencing specific patch coordinates, and region-based question answering, where the user draws a box on the image and asks about its contents.

O C R and document understanding. Because the V i T processes the image patch by patch, models can learn to read text in images. A screenshot of code, a photograph of a receipt, a page of a textbook. But how well this works depends heavily on resolution, training data, and fine-tuning. A model trained primarily on natural photographs may struggle with dense text documents. Models fine-tuned on document-heavy datasets, with high-resolution tiling to capture small text, perform much better. The capability is not free. It requires deliberate investment in the right training data and resolution handling.

Video understanding. Video extends image understanding by processing multiple frames. The simplest approach is to sample key frames from the video, encode each as a sequence of visual tokens, and concatenate them. This works for short clips but is expensive for long videos, since each frame adds hundreds of visual tokens. More sophisticated approaches use temporal pooling across frames, only encoding the differences between consecutive frames, or using specialized video encoders that capture motion directly.

Audio is another modality that follows a similar pattern. An audio encoder, often a Whisper-style model, converts audio into a sequence of embeddings. These are projected into the language model's space and processed alongside text tokens. Gemini and G P T four o process audio natively this way.

Let me address a question that comes up often. Is the vision encoder necessary? Could you just feed raw pixel values into the language model? In principle, yes. You could treat each pixel or each patch as a "token" and let the language model process it directly. Some research explores this, notably Fuyu from Adept, which feeds image patches directly into a decoder-only transformer without a separate vision encoder. The advantage is simplicity. The disadvantage is efficiency. A pretrained V i T compresses a two hundred and twenty-four pixel image from fifty thousand pixel values into a few hundred rich embedding vectors. Without that compression, the language model has to learn visual perception from scratch and process many more tokens per image.

Let me add more detail on the training stages for modern multimodal models, because the recipe has gotten more sophisticated since the original LLaVA.

The current best practice has three or four stages. Stage one is connector pretraining. Freeze both the vision encoder and the language model. Train only the projection layer, the connector, on large-scale image-caption pairs. This stage is cheap because only a small number of parameters are being updated. It teaches the connector to translate between the two representation spaces.

Stage two is visual instruction tuning. Unfreeze the language model and optionally the vision encoder. Fine-tune on a curated dataset of visual conversations, question-answer pairs about images, diagram interpretation, chart reading, and multi-image reasoning. This stage is where the model learns to actually do useful things with visual input, not just align representations.

Stage three, which is increasingly common, is high-resolution and document-specific tuning. Earlier stages may use relatively low resolution, two hundred and twenty-four or three hundred and thirty-six pixels, to keep the visual token count manageable. Stage three introduces high-resolution processing, often using a tiling strategy. The image is divided into tiles at the original resolution, each tile is processed independently by the vision encoder, and the resulting embeddings are concatenated. This lets the model see fine detail, small text, dense diagrams, subtle visual features, at the cost of many more visual tokens per image. Models fine-tuned with high-resolution tiling on document-heavy datasets show dramatically better performance on O C R, table extraction, and code screenshot understanding.

Stage four, for frontier models, is post-training with R L H F or D P O using multimodal preference data. Human evaluators compare the model's responses to visual queries, and the preference signal shapes the model's visual reasoning just as it shapes text reasoning. This stage is what makes the difference between a model that can describe images and a model that can thoughtfully analyze them.

The trajectory of the field suggests that late fusion with a pretrained vision encoder will remain dominant, but the boundary between "vision encoder" and "language model" is blurring. Some models fine-tune the vision encoder jointly. Others use lightweight encoders that do less compression, pushing more visual reasoning into the language model's attention layers. The trend is toward tighter integration without giving up the benefits of pretrained components.

To summarize the arc. Vision was added to language models not by reinventing the architecture but by treating images as token sequences and letting the existing attention mechanism do the work. C L I P aligned vision and language representations. LLaVA showed that a simple projection layer is enough to connect them. And the multi-stage training recipe, connector pretraining, visual instruction tuning, high-resolution adaptation, and multimodal R L H F, is what turns a proof of concept into a production-quality multimodal system.

In the next episode, we leave architecture behind and enter the post-training world. A pretrained model, no matter how capable, is just a next-token predictor. It does not follow instructions, it does not refuse harmful requests, it does not behave like a chatbot. Turning it into something useful requires a pipeline. Supervised instruction tuning, then reinforcement learning from human feedback. That pipeline, and the problems it solves and creates, is what we will cover next.
