# Episode 6: Long context

Welcome to episode six. In episode one we talked about the quadratic cost of attention. In episode three we covered R o P E and why it extrapolates better than learned position embeddings, but still degrades at positions far beyond the training context. In episode four we covered the K V cache and how it dominates inference memory for long sequences. All three topics converge on one practical problem. How do you make a model work on sequences much longer than it was trained on?

The demand for long context is enormous and growing. You want to feed an entire codebase into a model. Or a hundred-page legal document. Or a two-hour meeting transcript. Or an entire book. The models of twenty twenty two had context windows of two thousand to four thousand tokens. By twenty twenty five, Gemini handles a million tokens, and Claude handles two hundred thousand. That is a hundred-fold or more increase in just three years. Today we are going to cover the techniques that made this possible, often without retraining from scratch. N T K scaling, Y a R N, sliding-window attention, and attention sinks.

Let me start with the R o P E extrapolation problem, because R o P E context extension is the first thing you reach for when you need a longer context window.

As we covered in episode three, R o P E encodes position by rotating query and key vectors at frequencies determined by a base parameter theta. The rotation angle at position m for dimension pair i is m times theta to the power of negative two i over d. Low-dimension pairs rotate quickly, high-dimension pairs rotate slowly. The base theta is typically ten thousand.

During training, the model sees positions zero through L minus one, where L is the training context length. For each dimension pair, the model sees a specific range of angles. The fast-rotating pairs complete many full cycles within L positions. The model has experienced all possible angles for these pairs, and they generalize well to any position. But the slow-rotating pairs may complete only a fraction of a full cycle during training. A pair with a period of, say, sixty thousand positions will only sweep through one-twelfth of its cycle during training on a four-thousand-token context.

At inference time, if you process a sequence longer than L, these slow pairs produce angles the model never encountered. The attention scores, which depend on the dot products of rotated queries and keys, produce values outside the range the model learned to work with. The result is degraded attention patterns and lower output quality.

The simplest fix is linear position interpolation, proposed by Chen and colleagues at Meta in twenty twenty three. The idea is to scale down all position indices so they fit within the original training range. If the model was trained at context length four thousand and you want to handle thirty-two thousand tokens, multiply all position indices by four thousand over thirty-two thousand, which is one-eighth. Position thirty-two thousand becomes position four thousand. Every position now maps to an angle the model has seen.

This works, but it has a well-defined failure mode. You have compressed the entire position space. Positions zero and one, which were previously one unit apart, are now one-eighth of a unit apart. The model's ability to distinguish nearby positions degrades. Fine-grained local position information, like whether token A is immediately before or after token B, becomes harder to resolve. This is not a problem for the slow-rotating pairs, because they were not very sensitive to local position differences anyway. But for the fast-rotating pairs, which specifically encode local position relationships, the compression throws away important resolution.

N T K-aware scaling, proposed by the Reddit user bloc97 in mid twenty twenty three, addresses this elegantly. N T K stands for Neural Tangent Kernel, though the connection to actual N T K theory is loose and historical. The key idea is to scale the frequencies non-uniformly rather than scaling the positions uniformly.

Here is the intuition. R o P E uses a spectrum of frequencies across the embedding dimensions. Think of it as a set of clocks, some ticking fast and some ticking slow. The slow clocks capture long-range position information. They need to be adjusted for longer contexts because they encounter positions where they produce unfamiliar angles. The fast clocks capture local position information. They do not need adjustment because they have already experienced all possible angle values during training, since they complete many full cycles even in a short context.

N T K scaling achieves this by increasing the base parameter theta. Instead of theta equals ten thousand, you use a larger value, like one hundred thousand or five hundred thousand. Increasing theta stretches all the frequencies, making all the clocks tick slower. But the effect is proportionally larger on the already-slow clocks. The fastest pairs are still fast enough to resolve local positions. The slowest pairs are now slow enough that their angles at position thirty-two thousand fall within the range they covered at position four thousand with the original theta.

The formula scales theta by a factor alpha, where alpha is chosen based on the desired context extension ratio and the model dimension. Specifically, alpha equals the extension ratio raised to the power of d over d minus two, where d is the embedding dimension. For practical purposes, the key point is that you are making one change, increasing theta, and it mostly affects the long-range end of the frequency spectrum while leaving the short-range end approximately intact.

Y a R N, which stands for Yet Another R o P E Extension Method, published by Peng and colleagues in late twenty twenty three, builds on N T K scaling with three refinements.

First, Y a R N uses a per-dimension interpolation scheme rather than a uniform theta change. It categorizes each dimension pair into three groups based on its frequency relative to the training context length. High-frequency pairs, those that complete many cycles during training, are left unmodified. Low-frequency pairs, those that barely complete a cycle, are linearly interpolated, similar to the Chen approach. Medium-frequency pairs get a blended treatment, partly interpolated and partly left alone. The boundaries and blending are computed automatically from the training context length and the target extension ratio. This three-way treatment gives finer control than N T K scaling, which applies the same theta change to all dimensions.

Second, Y a R N includes an attention temperature correction. When you change the R o P E frequencies, the scale of the dot products in attention changes subtly, because the distribution of angles shifts. If the angles are more concentrated, the dot products tend to be larger, producing sharper attention distributions after softmax. If more spread out, softer distributions. Y a R N adjusts the attention logits by a scaling factor that compensates for this shift, keeping the effective temperature of the attention consistent with what the model was trained on.

Third, Y a R N applies a small amount of fine-tuning on long-context data after changing the R o P E parameters. This is typically a few hundred training steps on documents that are longer than the original training context. The fine-tuning allows the model to adapt to the new frequency distribution rather than relying entirely on the geometric arguments for why the change should work. In practice, this fine-tuning step is short and cheap, but it closes the remaining quality gap between the extended model and a model trained from scratch at the longer context length.

Both N T K and Y a R N, and related approaches like Code Llama's interpolation, share a crucial practical property. They do not require retraining from scratch. You take an existing trained model, modify the R o P E parameters, do a small amount of fine-tuning, and the model handles much longer contexts. This is why rapid context extensions became possible. Within weeks of Llama two's release, with its four-thousand-token context, community fine-tunes were operating at thirty-two thousand and sixty-four thousand tokens using these techniques. The base model weights are reused. Only the position encoding is adjusted.

Now let me turn to sliding-window attention, which attacks the long-context problem from the attention mechanism side rather than the position encoding side.

Standard full attention is quadratic. Every position attends to every other position, giving n squared attention scores per head per layer. At sequence length one hundred and twenty-eight thousand, that is over sixteen billion scores per head. Even with flash attention making the computation memory-efficient, the raw F L O P count is enormous.

Sliding-window attention observes that most attention is local. When you analyze trained attention patterns, you find that the vast majority of attention mass falls on tokens within a few thousand positions. Long-range attention exists but is sparse. Most tokens primarily care about their recent context. So instead of computing all n squared scores, each token only attends to the previous W tokens, where W is the window size.

The attention computation at each layer becomes n times W instead of n squared. For a sequence of one hundred and twenty-eight thousand tokens with a window of four thousand, that is a factor of thirty-two reduction in compute per layer. For even longer sequences, the savings are proportionally larger, because the window size is fixed while full attention would grow quadratically.

The natural concern is that limiting attention to a local window loses long-range information. And within a single layer, it does. A token at position ten thousand cannot directly attend to a token at position one thousand if the window is four thousand. But information propagates through layers. In layer one, position ten thousand sees positions six thousand through ten thousand. After layer one, the representation at position ten thousand contains information about those positions. In layer two, that enriched representation attends to its window again, but the representations within that window already carry information from their own windows in the previous layer. The effective receptive field grows layer by layer.

After L layers with window W, information can propagate across L times W positions. For a model with thirty-two layers and a window of four thousand, the theoretical receptive field is one hundred and twenty-eight thousand positions. This is the effective context length, achieved with linear per-layer cost.

Mistral, released in September twenty twenty three, was the first major model to use sliding-window attention. It used a window of four thousand and ninety-six tokens across thirty-two layers, giving an effective receptive field of over one hundred and thirty thousand positions. It performed comparably to models with full attention on most benchmarks while being significantly more efficient on long sequences.

Some architectures interleave sliding-window and full-attention layers. Every fourth or every eighth layer uses full attention, which has quadratic cost but provides true global information flow. The remaining layers use sliding-window attention, which is cheap. This hybrid approach gives the model periodic opportunities for global attention while keeping the average cost much lower than full attention everywhere. Gemma two from Google DeepMind uses this interleaved pattern.

Sliding-window attention also simplifies the K V cache, as we discussed in episode four. With full attention, the K V cache grows without bound as the sequence gets longer. Every new token adds its key and value vectors to the cache, and no old entries can be removed because any future token might attend to any past token. With sliding-window attention, each token only attends to the last W tokens, so you only need to cache the most recent W key-value pairs per layer. Old entries can be evicted once they fall outside the window. The cache has a fixed maximum size regardless of sequence length.

Now, attention sinks. This is both an empirical phenomenon and a technique for stabilizing long-context inference, and it is more subtle than the methods above.

In twenty twenty three, Xiao and colleagues at M I T published the StreamingLLM paper, which documented a curious behavior in trained language models. Across many different models, the first few tokens in the sequence receive a disproportionate amount of attention, regardless of what those tokens actually contain. It could be a beginning-of-sequence token, a space, an arbitrary word. The attention heads consistently allocate significant attention mass to these initial positions. The paper calls this phenomenon "attention sinks."

The likely explanation connects to how softmax attention works. Every attention head must produce weights that sum to one, because of the softmax normalization. When no other token in the context is particularly relevant to the current query, the model still has to put the attention mass somewhere. The first token, which is always present and always at the same position, becomes a convenient dump. It is the default destination for attention that has nowhere else to go. The model learns during training that it is safe to attend to this position when it has nothing specific to look for, because the content at position zero is predictable and attending to it does not introduce harmful information.

Why does this matter for long-context inference? Consider a system that uses a fixed-size K V cache to handle arbitrarily long streams of text, like a chatbot processing an hours-long conversation. At some point, the cache fills up and old tokens must be evicted. The natural eviction policy is to remove the oldest tokens, keeping the most recent W tokens. But when you evict the first token, the model loses its attention sink. Future attention computations no longer have a safe default position, and the attention patterns become erratic. Perplexity spikes. Output quality degrades sharply.

The fix is simple and elegant. Keep the first few tokens in the K V cache permanently. Evict middle tokens as needed, but always retain the first four or so positions. These serve as stable attention sinks that never get evicted. The rest of the cache operates as a sliding window over recent tokens. This tiny change, pinning a handful of entries at the start of the cache, allows fixed-size K V caches to work reliably for arbitrarily long sequences.

Some newer models take this a step further by explicitly training with a dedicated sink token. Instead of relying on whatever happens to be the first token of the sequence, the model is trained with a special purpose-built token at position zero that is designed to absorb excess attention. This cleans up the attention patterns and makes the sink behavior more consistent across different inputs.

Let me put all of these techniques together. The long-context toolkit as of twenty twenty five is a stack of complementary approaches. R o P E provides the position encoding foundation. N T K and Y a R N extend R o P E to longer contexts by adjusting the frequency spectrum. Sliding-window attention reduces the per-layer cost from quadratic to linear in the window size. Full-attention layers, interleaved with sliding-window layers, provide periodic global information flow. Attention sinks stabilize inference with bounded K V caches. Flash attention makes whatever attention pattern you choose memory-efficient. G Q A keeps the K V cache small per token. These compose. A modern long-context model might use R o P E with theta extended to five hundred thousand, G Q A with eight K V groups, sliding-window attention with periodic full layers, attention sink tokens, and flash attention throughout.

There is one more phenomenon worth covering, because it affects how you actually use long-context models in practice. It is called lost in the middle, documented by Liu and colleagues at Stanford in twenty twenty three. They found that when you place relevant information at different positions within a long context, the model's ability to find and use that information is not uniform. Models are best at using information near the beginning of the context and near the end. Information placed in the middle of a long document is significantly less likely to be retrieved and used correctly.

This is a striking finding because it means that having a hundred-thousand-token context window does not mean the model attends equally to all hundred thousand tokens. The primacy effect, attention to the beginning, is partly explained by attention sinks, the model has learned to attend strongly to early positions. The recency effect, attention to the end, is partly because the most recent tokens are closest in the causal attention window and receive the strongest positional signal through R o P E. The middle gets squeezed.

The practical implication is that the order in which you present information in a long context matters. If you are stuffing a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline's results into the context, putting the most relevant chunks at the beginning or end, rather than burying them in the middle, measurably improves accuracy. Some systems explicitly reorder their retrieved documents to exploit this bias.

This also connects to a broader question about whether long context is always the right approach. Retrieval-augmented generation, or R A G, is the alternative. Instead of putting everything into the context and hoping the model finds the relevant parts, you use a retrieval system, often a vector database with embedding-based search, to find the most relevant chunks first, and put only those chunks into a short context. R A G uses much less compute per query, avoids the lost-in-the-middle problem, and works with models that have shorter context windows. The tradeoff is that retrieval can miss relevant information if the query does not match the right chunks, and the chunking itself can break important cross-document connections. Long context and R A G are complementary, not competing, and many production systems use both, retrieving relevant chunks and placing them in a long context alongside other material.

The frontier continues to push. Ring attention distributes the sequence across multiple G P Us and streams K V cache segments between them, enabling even longer sequences by parallelizing across devices. Infini-attention from Google introduces a compressed memory mechanism that stores information from earlier parts of the sequence in a fixed-size state, allowing the model to access arbitrarily old information without storing the full K V cache. Various quantization techniques compress the K V cache entries from sixteen bits to four or even two bits per element, trading a small quality loss for a four to eight times reduction in cache memory.
